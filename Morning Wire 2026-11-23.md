## Lead: The Army tells a federal court it will act anyway

**The facts.** The US Army says it will detonate 68 barrels — roughly four to five tons — of lead azide at a munitions plant in Augusta, Iowa, by Tuesday, under emergency legal powers that let it proceed despite a federal appeals court order pausing its permit (Reuters, AP, CNN, NBC, USA Today, Al Jazeera). The administration told a court this over the weekend. In the same filing cycle the Justice Department asked the appeals court to lift its own pause.

**Why it leads.** Both halves of that sentence matter, and they point in opposite directions. Asking the court to lift the stay is the ordinary fudge — appeal, execution usually follows. Telling the court, in the same breath, that the executive has a statutory power to proceed regardless is a different claim: it is the assertion that the order does not bind. Our rolling gate on court-order compliance names exactly this as the tell — "the first order disobeyed rather than appealed." Today is the first time in this cycle the executive has put both in writing at once. Until now the pattern was delay, re-rule, and moot the case. The pattern now includes a claimed right to act through the order.

**Why the substance is small and the form is not.** Lead azide is a toxic, shock-sensitive detonating compound; the county, the state and residents have fought the disposal for months, and the risk argument on both sides is real. That is what makes it the ideal carrier: nobody can accuse the Army of acting on a political target, because the target is a barrel. The instrument being tested is not immigration or election law, it is whether a district court's pause can be walked through by invoking emergency authority. An emergency power asserted once, on a sympathetic fact pattern, is available on the next one.

**The pattern.** A court order is a claim on the future: comply while the case proceeds. An executive that says "we will comply with the litigation and act anyway" has not defied the court — it has reclassified the court as a comment period. Watch which of the two sentences survives contact with the appeals court. If the court accepts the emergency-power argument, the door is open without anyone having to break it.

**What history says.** The 1832 Supreme Court decision in Worcester v. Georgia settled that the Cherokee were a nation with treaty rights; the state ignored it, and the executive declined to enforce it. Jackson's reported line — "John Marshall has made his decision, now let him enforce it" — is remembered because it made visible the thing that usually stays invisible: a court with no marshal of its own. Every successful act of executive non-compliance in American history has had the same shape: not a refusal on the merits, but a finding that the order was never the operative instrument. The 1974 subpoena fight is the counterexample, and it ended in resignation precisely because the order *was* the instrument.

**What we're watching next.** The appeals court's ruling on the stay. A grant that reaches the emergency-powers question is the important document, not the one that lifts the pause on narrow grounds. And the detonation itself: whether it happens Tuesday, and whether anything follows it — a contempt filing, a marshal, or silence.

**What I am not doing.** Not scoring this as the D1 firing event. Our own gate's firing condition is an order declined and unenforced for thirty days. The order is contested, the appeal is live, and the deadline the Army named is inside the window. This is the first clean test of the channel, not the result.

## 3. Pantego, Texas: one arrest, one hundred thousand dollars

**The facts.** A ProPublica–Texas Tribune investigation (20 November, carried by TTPM today) reports that the police department of Pantego, a North Texas town of about 2,500 people, received $100,000 in federal funding after making a single immigration arrest in June under a 287(g) agreement. The chief told the town council the department could leave the programme without penalty and keep the equipment; all it had to do was make one arrest. Between late April and mid-June it made two. The Smith County Sheriff's Office received nearly $2.2 million through the programme from January 2025 to June 2026, the most of any department reviewed. There are 429 agencies in the programme.

**Why this is the item and why it came from pass two.** It never led a national board; it surfaced in the investigations sweep and it is the highest-value item of the morning. A per-arrest payment converts an enforcement agreement into a revenue line. Once a small department can fund a fifth to two-fifths of its budget with immigration arrests, the decision to arrest stops being a legal judgment about a person and becomes a budget question about the month. Nothing in the arrangement requires a quota, and nothing has to: the price does the work.

**The pattern.** This is the contracted-coercion series reaching its market form. First the federal government deputised local officers; then it paid them per head; the last step, which this story documents, is that the payment is large relative to the department's own budget. That is not a subsidy, it is a business model, and business models scale without orders.

**What history says.** The post-Reconstruction convict-lease system and the 1990s asset-forfeiture boom both ran on the same mechanism: a public function with a private revenue stream attached, where the volume of enforcement tracks the revenue rather than the offence. Forfeiture was eventually curbed by state statutes requiring conviction first — a fix aimed at the money, not at the police.

**What we're watching next.** Whether any Texas municipality publishes the payment schedule, and whether a state legislator introduces a per-arrest prohibition. The contract language is the document: a clause allowing exit without clawback is what makes the arrangement a grant rather than a purchase, and it is why the incentive persists after the politics turns.

## 4. Iran: the blockade widens, and Tehran says it will use "all available tools"

**The facts.** Iran's foreign ministry spokesman, Esmaeil Baghaei, told a weekly press conference that Iran will use all available tools to confront the US naval blockade (Reuters, 23 November). The blockade itself was widened on 19–20 November to cover any vessel taking Iranian cargo by ship-to-ship transfer, with the Joint Maritime Information Center warning that recipients face military intervention (USNI, Al Jazeera, Business Standard). Trump said on 22 November that Iran's oil exports had been cut off completely and dared Tehran to breach the blockade.

**Why it matters.** The blockade has crossed the line from intercepting ships bound to and from Iran to policing the water for cargo that has already left an Iranian hull. That is a much wider net, and it is the kind that produces incidents, because the ship being stopped is now frequently not Iranian and not bound for Iran. Every one of those is a flag state, an insurer and a lawyer.

**The pattern.** Escalation by administrative widening rather than by strike. The named operational phase our Iran tree waits for is still absent; what keeps arriving instead is the same campaign with its scope redefined in a notice to mariners. This is the third widening since the war began and it has never required an announcement from the Pentagon, which is why posture keeps rising without the threshold being met.

**What history says.** The 1962 quarantine of Cuba is the reference case for a blockade that worked: it was narrow, it was announced, and it was backed by an explicit threat that the other side understood. The 1980s Tanker War in the same water is the warning: a wide, slow, low-visibility interdiction campaign produced years of shipping attacks, reflagging and great-power escort duties without ever producing a decision. The difference between the two is not force level. It is whether the other side can tell what would end it.

**Card effect.** `iran-escalation-node1-fires` holds at 56. A blockade expansion is policy, not a named operational phase; Iran's "all tools" is a threat of response, not a phase of its own. Both were already inside the existing tempo. The thing that would move this card is the administration's own noun.

## 6. The courts moved in two directions this weekend

**The facts.** A federal appeals court held that the federal law requiring accommodations for pregnant workers is unconstitutional (AP). Separately, the Army's move in Iowa (item 1) is the executive asserting a power to act despite a pause.

**Why it matters.** One item is a court voiding a statute Congress passed; the other is an executive claiming to proceed past a court. Both are the same weekend's answer to the same question — which branch's word is final — and they point opposite ways. An environment in which both happen within 48 hours is not a hierarchy breaking down in one direction; it is a hierarchy in which each branch is testing the others' limits at once.

**What history says.** The 1937 court-packing fight began with a judiciary striking New Deal statutes and ended with a judiciary that stopped. The mechanism was not the bill, which failed. It was that the political cost of continued invalidation rose above the cost of deference. The same arithmetic now runs in reverse: a judiciary that voids a Democratic-passed statute while an executive walks past its orders is a judiciary whose legitimacy is being spent from both ends at once.
