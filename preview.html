What this board is measuring

Our model rests on what we call Material-Generational Enclosure Theory. Western history, on this account, is a long struggle between two forces: one that encloses human life — turning people into things that can be measured, owned and sold — and one that resists it, the drive for liberty, autonomy and rights. The enclosing force keeps changing shape: slavery, in which the body itself was the property; feudalism, in which land and inherited status were; capitalism, in which labour, money and attention were. It is mutating again now.

Roughly every eighty to a hundred years the pattern reaches a crisis point — what Strauss and Howe call a turning. A turning is what this struggle looks like in its acute phase. Naming the mechanism that drives the recurring cycle, rather than merely describing its rhythm, is the aim of the model.

MGET is a unification of three things that were never joined. It keeps Strauss and Howe's phase language, which describes the cycle but cannot explain it. It takes Turchin's measurable drivers of collapse — elite overproduction, fiscal strain, the erosion of state legitimacy — as the engine that moves the cycle. And it takes Polanyi's double movement, the counter-pull that enclosure always provokes, as the shape of the resistance. Joined and instrumented, they give the cycle a quantitative footing it has never had.

The theory is built to be wrong in public. If the cycle is driven by enclosure, it should be possible to say in advance what a turning looks like before it arrives, and to name what would count as evidence against the claim. Our instrument does this with four legs, each carrying a number, a measurement window and a stated condition that would falsify it. A turning that cannot be distinguished from ordinary institutional wear, a resistance that fails to appear where enclosure is deepest, or a crisis that arrives with no prior loss of legitimacy — each of these would count against the model rather than for it. That is a stricter standard than the genre is used to. It is also the only kind of claim worth making about the future.

Model updates

Nothing was deleted from the record: the thesis pile is still counted, the wire ledger is still here, and the forecast register still scores in public. What was deleted is one card that explained the page to itself.

What we learned from the old model

Version 1 of this board was built on a thesis and it presented its readings as measurements. Running it in public killed one of its load-bearing claims and demoted two more, and the honest thing to do with that is say it on the front page rather than bury it in a change log. The turning-window calendar does not survive its first out-of-sample test — it stays on the board as framing and a live hypothesis, no longer as a confirmed timing instrument. The continental-windows claim is withdrawn, because the check that was supposed to validate the calendar independently turned out to be measuring an artefact. Window independence is refuted: inside English history a window that brackets an event is close to a definition, not a discovery. Two things were rebuilt rather than dropped — legs 1 and 2 now run on external baselines instead of hand-set anchors, and the docking test gives leg 4 a real kill condition. What all of it bought is a board that can say where it is blind instead of quietly filling the gap.

2 · The MGET model — macro to micro

2.1 Macro · model state

Four numbers, four different questions — and they are not on the same scale, so they cannot be added, averaged or ranked against each other. Read them as a set, left to right: how much of the model is firing (a count out of four), how hard the state is pressing (enforcement events per hundred entries written), how captured the institutions already are (an average out of 100), and how fast they are withdrawing their own standing (withdrawals per month).

Higher is worse in all four, which is the one property they share. Beyond that they move on different clocks: the leg count changes only at phase boundaries, the enforcement rate moves month to month, the institutional average moves on a weekly review, and the withdrawal rate is a two-month level with no baseline behind it. A reading in one box is not corroboration for a reading in another — four numbers rising together in the same week is a coincidence until the windows are checked.

What the row is for is a single question: is the pressure being resisted? Legs fired and enforcement flow describe the pressure. Institutional pressure and withdrawals describe what is left of the capacity to resist. The model's claim is that these separate. When enforcement is high and withdrawals are flat, the state is pressing and the institutions are complying. When withdrawals start climbing while enforcement climbs, the resistance has begun to show.

A stock says how captured an institution is today; a flow says whether the audience that would have to accept force is being prepared faster than it was last week. The second is the leading indicator, and it never belonged in the dial table — you cannot defend "dehumanization is 71" from public observables, but you can defend "33 specimens this week, 45 coded right-flank, 25 reciprocal."

3.4 Against the model — is Absorption converting?
The model reads Absorption at 76 on a stock basis. The flow says the rate stepped up 4× into the election and did not return to baseline after it. That combination is the one the stock cannot express: high stock, rising flow means the frame is being refreshed, not merely retained. It is the leading edge of the M reading, and it is the only reading on this page that updates daily.
How to read it, plainly. Big number, bad. Rising, worse. Rising after a defeat means the frame is being used to explain the defeat rather than to contest it — the cheap version, and it still works. Falling after a defeat is the repair succeeding. The blind spot is on the panel, not hidden: capture is opportunistic. Samuel captures what the feed serves him. The rate is indicative, not a census, and the direction split is trustworthy only because the watch is deliberately symmetric. Do not quote a specimen count as a percentage of anything.
What would falsify this block. If a full-feed sample, not the owner's feed, shows the W41 peak is a sampler artefact, the rate series is void and only the direction coding survives. Until then the rate is a trend line on a non-random sample, labelled as one.

What this changes in the model

The four legs are not four of the same thing, and scoring them the same way is the defect this table exposes. Legs 1–3 are pressure — continuous state variables, chronically elevated in any late-modern society, useful as an index of strain and useless as a classifier. Leg 4 is conversion — the variable that decides whether stored pressure discharges into a rupture or vents as reform. Turchin's own structure has the same asymmetry: elite overproduction is a slow structural trend across whole centuries, while elite disunity and legitimation failure are what cluster at the break. From this build the legs are scored separately: a pressure index (mean of legs 1–3, continuous, no threshold claims) and a conversion read (leg 4 band). Case-level falsifier: if leg 4 quiet cases begin rupturing as the 2026 window resolves, the selector is dead and the legs revert to a single pooled score.

What is still missing. Only 6 of 25 cases are ruptures, and the control set is not a random sample — it is the cases the desk chose to run, which over-weights crises it found interesting. The base rates above are base rates of this panel, not of history. Filling that needs quiet windows drawn on a rule (every fifth year, say) rather than on interest, which needs series for legs 1–3 that go back far enough — the leg 1 and leg 2 external series start in 1948 and 2000 respectively, so anything before 1948 is being scored off desk judgement, and the table says so by leaving the loads blank where the case file has no score.

3.8 · The panel is an S&H panel — and that changes what it can prove new, 2026-11-06

Correction to 3.7. The 25 control cases were not picked for being interesting crises; they were drawn from the Strauss & Howe saeculum calendar, which is the thing the whole apparatus exists to test. That is a stronger bias than interest, and a different one: the cases were selected on the theory's own prediction of when regimes break. Twenty-two of twenty-five map onto a turning, and every canonical Fourth Turning in the Anglo-American sequence is present (1773–94, 1860–65, 1929–46, 2008–). So the P(rupture) column in 3.7 is partly a property of the inclusion rule, not a finding about history. A panel built from a crisis list will find crises.

What the panel can do is the thing worth doing. Selection was on S&H's window, not on the leg readings — so any discrimination the legs achieve inside a predicted crisis window is not contaminated by how the cases were chosen. That is the one non-circular test available here, and it is the test MGET has to pass to earn a place beside S&H rather than under it.

If the window closes with leg 4 never fired and the regime nonetheless breaks, the leg-4 selector is dead.

The limit, stated plainly. This panel cannot test whether turnings exist. Testing S&H requires windows drawn by rule without reference to the calendar — a fixed-year draw (every fifth year, say) scored blind, plus S&H's own non-crisis predictions as matched controls. Until that exists, the honest claim is narrow: within windows S&H named as crises, a leg-4 reading separates the ones that resolved the regime question from the ones that did not. That is a claim about a supplement to the theory, not a proof of the theory.

3.9 · The blind draw — what the calendar is actually worth working tool, 2026-11-06

The test that was missing. 3.8 could only test discrimination inside windows S&H named. 5 · Registers — what each pile can and cannot tell you
A forecast is a bet: a claim with a price, a deadline and two possible endings. It is the only pile that can be right or wrong, so it is the only pile that scores.
An accumulation is a tally: no deadline, no resolution, just a count that goes up. Useful as a measurement, useless as a prediction — a tally can never be wrong.
A thesis is a frame: an argument about how the system works. No deadline either, so nothing settles it, so it sits there burning attention while looking unfinished.
RegisterCardsResolvedRateWhat a low rate means
Rate is resolved divided by the cards that can score. A low rate in the forecast register is a result and it costs something. A low rate in the thesis register is an instruction: the claim was never written in a form the world could contradict, so the fix is to rewrite it as a bet or retire it.
The distinction that decides the number. Count every resolution in the register and the rate reads one thing; count only the cards that could be scored honestly and it reads another. This line is computed from the export on every build, so it cannot drift from the table above.
