2 · Three registers, not one list

What this section is actually saying

Everything the board tracks gets written down as a "card" — one claim, one date, one way to be wrong. The problem is that not all cards are the same kind of thing, and the board lists them as if they were. This section splits them into three piles by what they can ever do.
Register A — Forecast. A bet. "X happens by date Y." It has a price (a probability), a deadline, and two possible endings: it hits or it misses. This is the only pile that can score.
Register B — Accumulation. A tally. "This many things of this kind happened." It has no deadline and no way to be wrong — it just counts up. Useful as a measure, useless as a prediction. It cannot score, and pretending otherwise would be a lie.
Register C — Thesis. A frame. "Here is the shape of what's coming." It has no deadline either, so nothing ever settles it, so it sits on the page forever looking unfinished.
How to read the table: Cards = how many are in the pile. Resolved = how many have reached an ending. Rate = resolved ÷ cards. A pile with a low rate is not necessarily bad work — but a pile at 0% is not being tested at all, and that is a fact about how the claim was written, not about the world. Register C sits at 0 of 28, and that number is the single most useful thing on this page: it tells you where to spend the next hour.

3 · What is feeding each leg — the instrument

Why this section exists

M, G, E and T are the four legs of the model — Material, Governance, Enclosure, Turning. Each carries a load (0–100, how much pressure is on that leg) and a state (fired / absent). The state was argued case by case. The load was not: three of the four numbers were set by feel with no stated anchors, so nothing could ever move them down and no reader could check them. This section is the missing instrument — four anchors per leg, each scored 0–5 on the same written scale, load = (sum ÷ 4) × 20. Equal weights, published anchors, and a number that falls when an anchor falls.
The scale — identical for every anchor, every leg:
0 — nothing there; absent on the record
1 — rhetoric only; talk with no act behind it
2 — an act, isolated, no second instance
3 — an act plus a standing mechanism that repeats it
4 — the mechanism is the default; it runs without being asked
5 — complete, irreversible, something else has taken over
The rule that makes anchors work: an anchor must be a thing you can find, not a judgement about how bad it feels. "ALPR procurement consolidation" can be checked; "enclosure is bad" cannot. Where a leg's definition names fewer than four anchors, the gap is filled and marked as added — E needed one, T needed all four.

Status: provisional. One coder, not blind, n=1. Load here transmits relative movement, not absolute truth, and cannot be used to file or settle a card until coded blind twice.

4 · Byte budget — and what "byte" means

Plainly, for a reader who has never thought about this

A byte is roughly one character — a letter, a digit, a space, a full stop. The word "the" plus its space is 4 bytes. This paragraph is about 300 bytes.

A kilobyte (KB) is about a thousand of those. The dashboard page is 290 KB — about 290,000 characters, which is roughly a 100-page paperback. Every time anyone opens the board, their device downloads that whole paperback before it can draw the first line.
Why it matters, three reasons, in the order a reader feels them:

1. It's slow. 290 KB on a phone is several seconds of blank screen and a slice of someone's data plan, every single visit.

2. It's the wrong shape. Half the page is one list — the forecast register at 145 KB. And of that list, 28 cards are theses that can never resolve, so they will still be there, word for word, in six months. The page gets heavier as it ages without getting more informative.

3. It hides the good part. The model block at the top is 7 KB. It's 2.4% of the page. The thing worth reading is a rounding error in the thing being downloaded.
The newspaper analogy: imagine a paper where the front page is excellent, and pages 2 to 50 reprint, in full, every claim the paper has ever made that it never checked. Nobody would call that thorough. They'd call it unread.

The fix is not deletion. Nothing is thrown away or softened. The 28 thesis cards move one click away into an annex; the 58 forecast cards stay inline; the 41 T-leg cards, which describe a leg the model scores absent, fold by default with their count still printed in the header. The board keeps every claim and loses 64 KB of scroll.

6 · Revision points

R1
Does the flow panel sit between state and stock, or after both as a footnote?
Between (mock as built) makes the derivation chain legible and gives E a computable delta. After keeps the two existing panels adjacent as they are today. Cost of “between”: the board's top three panels change at once, so one bad panel breaks the whole read.
R2
Do THESIS and MECHANISM keep a row on the public page at all?
Summary row only (mock): “28 theses under test, 0 resolved by design, converted to series.” Alternative: remove entirely and link the annex. I lean summary row — deleting them would hide that they were ever claimed.
R3
Are the 41 T-leg cards folded by default?
Yes in the mock, on the gauge protocol's own logic — card volume on a leg is not evidence about that leg, and T scores absent. Counter-argument: folding them hides the highest-pressure leg's work. Compromise: fold, but state the count in the panel header so the volume is visible without being led with.
R4
Weights 3 / 2 / 1 — keep, or drop weights entirely?
Keep and print (mock). Dropping weights makes the series a pure count — harder to dismiss as analyst judgement, but then Boston Dynamics robot dogs and the mass denaturalisation memo count the same. Third option: publish both numbers, count as headline, weight as secondary.
R5
Court blocks: negative score, or separate track?
Currently counted negative and reported separately, never netted. Keeping it that way means a green number can't be produced by winning in court. Alternative: a second rail on the same panel — “accrued” vs “stayed” — which is more informative but invites the reader to subtract.
R6
What is the baseline for “21”?
There is none before May 2026, so the page can only say “21 admitted, 13 channels, none closing” — never “high.” To fix properly means a backward census of 2024–2025 press and Federal Register output, which is a research task, not a build task. Until then the panel should carry the absence of a baseline as a visible label, not a footnote.
R7
Does the accumulation panel get a date it dies?
An instrument that cannot miss needs an expiry or it becomes permanent furniture. Proposal: rule sunsets 2027-11-05 unless re-adopted, and S-01's kill condition sits in the panel header, not the notes.
R8
Do we retire command.html?
87KB duplicate board, zero inbound links; index.html redirects to Internal Telemetry. Retire it. Separate small fix: a /space → data.json reconciliation guard, because 5 of 92 cards silently vanish from the export and one of them is a ROLLING card.
R9
Does T's anchor set survive someone who disagrees with my scoring?
I scored T2 (turnover) at 3 on the firing-leak series — 14 of 15 eventually leave, median 64 days — and called that turnover, not defection. A reasonable person scores it 4, which puts T at 45: still Straining, still absent, deck unchanged. Anchors are built to absorb disagreement without flipping the model, and that is the point of them. The number to watch is not the total, it is whether D2 ever moves — a replacement authority being articulated by an organised body. That single anchor is what decides whether T fires.
What this mock does not do: it is not wired to the data layer, so every figure above is hand-placed from today's census and will be stale tomorrow. It exists to settle the shape and R1–R9 before any of it touches sync_data.sh — because once the flow panel is live it has to pass the prod gate after every pull, and rewriting a panel that is already in production is how the other writer's build ends up served.
