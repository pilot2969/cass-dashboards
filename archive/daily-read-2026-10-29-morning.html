CARD STACK (newest first)

Federal prosecutors impanel a grand jury over one Senate race, five days before the election

What happened: The Justice Department has issued at least three dozen new subpoenas and document-preservation demands in its inquiry into the Alaska Senate race, seeking grand jury testimony, according to the Associated Press and NBC News, both citing an unnamed US official. The governor of Alaska, Mike Dunleavy, opened a separate state investigation on Wednesday. The President called again today for the Democratic candidate, Mary Peltola, to be prosecuted, and pressed his own administration to widen the inquiries. The underlying allegation, first reported by the New York Times, is that people working with her campaign helped recruit a second candidate also named Dan Sullivan into the race with the Republican incumbent.

Why it matters: A subpoena is not a charge. A preservation letter is not a finding. And “under criminal investigation” is a status a prosecutor can confer on anyone, unilaterally, without convincing a judge of anything. All three are being reported tonight in the register of established wrongdoing, and they are not the same thing. What is established is that the machinery is moving: a federal grand jury, a state investigator, and a President publicly demanding an indictment, aimed at one candidate, in the week her election is held.

The pattern: This board logged this morning that the case had crossed from a civil lawsuit into a criminal question inside a day. It crossed the rest of the way by tonight. The telling detail is that the legal predicate did not change while the apparatus did. The reported conduct is recruiting a namesake candidate; the most careful readings available — including the New York Times’ own account, which describes recruiting but not coercion — say that is not a crime. So the machinery is doing the work that the evidence is not.

What history says: Investigations opened in the final week of a campaign have a poor record of producing verdicts and a strong record of producing headlines. The 2016 and 2020 cycles both showed the pattern: a disclosed probe, a burst of coverage, and a resolution arriving too late to matter to the election it was about. What is different here is the compression — the probe is aimed at a candidate on the ballot, not at a concluded vote.

What we’re watching next: Whether any recipient of a subpoena is named, or any target letter confirmed. Whether the state and federal inquiries diverge in public, which would be the first genuinely exculpatory signal. And whether the grand jury returns anything before Nov 3, or is timed to land after it.

Sources: AP, NBC News, The Guardian, Alaska Public Media, Oct 28-29

A judge refuses to set aside 20,000 California ballots — and then appoints someone to examine the claim

What happened: The Justice Department has opened a criminal investigation in California into claims that thousands of noncitizens are registered to vote, and has begun subpoenaing election officials. Employees of the Los Angeles County registrar received grand jury subpoenas; Orange County’s registrar received one this week, in addition to three in September, seeking unredacted registration records for 77 people. On Wednesday a federal judge, David Carter, refused the administration’s request to order California to segregate and not count ballots from as many as 20,000 people the White House says are probably noncitizens. On Thursday he agreed to appoint a special master to evaluate the underlying claim.

Why it matters: Read the two rulings together and you get a hedge, not a win. The coercive remedy — set these ballots aside before they are counted — was denied. The factual claim was handed to an examiner. An examiner is also a clock, and five days out, a clock is the most useful instrument in the room. Meanwhile the subpoenas are producing exactly one durable product: a paper trail in the largest state, before the count.

The pattern: One state, one month, four instruments: criminal charges against a group of green-card holders, grand jury subpoenas to county registrars, a motion to segregate 20,000 ballots, and now a special master. No convictions. The volume is the message; the merits are still pending.

What history says: Judge Carter’s own words are the historical record worth keeping. He wrote that never before in American history has the federal government, on the eve of an election, sought to coerce a state and local government to abandon its own constitutional election procedures and bend to the will of the executive branch. Courts have repeatedly declined this request in other states this month. Noncitizen voting is documented to be very rare, and every case found so far has been found one person at a time, after the fact.

What we’re watching next: Whether the special master’s mandate carries a reporting date before or after certification. Whether any county is asked to sequester ballots. Whether the 77-name list becomes a template in other counties. And whether a third state appears within 48 hours — a third would mean this is a national template, not a California fight.

Sources: Bloomberg Law, The New York Times, The Guardian, court ruling in Carter, Oct 28-29

Chinese hackers pointed an AI agent at more than 200 organisations and got in to nine

What happened: CrowdStrike reported today that a China-linked group it calls VAPOR PANDA used an AI agent framework, which it names ARTEX, running on the DeepSeek model and a second commercial model, to probe more than 200 organisations across roughly 14 countries in 11 days. Nine were breached. South Korean financial institutions are among the confirmed victims. A human operator, the report says, steered the key decisions.

Why it matters: This is the first publicly documented case of AI being used as the volume layer of a real espionage campaign rather than a demonstration. The useful number is the one nobody is quoting: nine successes out of more than two hundred attempts. That is a low yield. The AI did not find a new way in; it ran the old ways in parallel, at a breadth a person cannot.

The pattern: Third time this month the same shape has appeared. The French supermarket refrigeration, the American base commissary freezers, and now this: the leverage is a shared control plane and a credential, not a clever exploit. One technician’s access, or one stolen key, multiplied across every system wired to the same cloud.

What history says: Every major automation of offence has produced the same two-part outcome — more attempts, similar success rates — from scripted worms through phishing kits to rented botnets. The scarce resource was never the number of attempts. It is the judgement about which door to push, and that stayed with the operator here, which is why the yield stayed low.

What we’re watching next: Whether a second security firm corroborates independently — single-vendor attribution is the weak link. Whether any victim is named beyond the South Korean financial sector. And the one to expect: whether this report is cited within a week as proof that AI attack is already here, in service of either loosening rules for AI companies or widening network-surveillance powers. Neither conclusion follows from nine breaches in two hundred tries.

Sources: CrowdStrike, NBC News, Infosecurity Magazine, Oct 29

Alphabet has its worst day ever, and the bond market rallies anyway

What happened: Alphabet fell hard enough after a revenue miss to record its worst single trading day as a public company, dragging the Nasdaq down 1.4 percent. Caterpillar dropped more than 9 percent despite beating expectations on both revenue and profit. The S&P 500 and the Dow each fell 0.3 percent. Health care, real estate and consumer staples rose, and most individual stocks finished higher. Inflation came in hot and growth came in strong, both close to forecasts. And the ten-year Treasury yield fell anyway, to 4.94 percent — down about a third of a point in a month, from a 24-year high.

Why it matters: That combination is the story. Strong data, hot inflation, and bonds rallying. When yields fall into good news, the money is not worried about the government’s credit; it is worried about the trade that has been carrying the index. The selloff is concentrated in artificial intelligence and the capital spending behind it. Everything defensive went up.

The pattern: This board has carried a question since September about whether the bond market would force a funding crisis — a six percent ten-year, a failed auction, a sovereign scare. That has not happened. For a month, through a widening war and hot inflation, the long end has been easing. The risk has relocated: from the government’s balance sheet to a single industry’s earnings, and it arrives in one session, not in a slow squeeze.

What history says: Momentum trades do not break on a single bad print; they break when the spending that justified them gets cut. The 2000 telecom unwind and the 2014 energy unwind both began as one company missing, then became a sector repricing when the capital expenditure guidance came down a quarter later. One down day is noise. Three is a repricing.

What we’re watching next: Whether the rotation holds past Friday. Whether hyperscalers cut capital spending guidance in the next earnings wave. Whether the ten-year stays below five percent through the election week — if it does, the rate-shock story is over for this cycle. And whether credit spreads widen; without that, this remains an equity event, not a systemic one.

Sources: Investopedia, CNBC, Trading Economics, Charles Schwab, AP, Oct 29

The general Hegseth fired is reported to have failed three lie-detector tests, and the case is now criminal

What happened: Lieutenant General Brett Sylvia, until this week the director for strategy, plans and policy on the Joint Staff — the Pentagon’s top military planner — was removed by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and has been referred to the Justice Department for possible prosecution. Reporting says he failed polygraph examinations during the leak inquiry, and that the disclosures at issue concerned both the war with Iran and the war in Ukraine. Hegseth described the conduct as severe misconduct.

Why it matters: The office that writes the options and the plan for a strike is empty while the President is weighing one. And the way the seat was emptied matters more than the seat: a lie-detector failure and a criminal referral tell every remaining officer in that directorate that talking to a reporter is a polygraph and a prosecutor. That is a chill on exactly the function a strike decision needs most — the part where someone says the option is worse than it looks.

The pattern: This is the same instrument this board has tracked across agencies all year, arriving at the Pentagon: the removal is not the point, the example is. Loyalty tests do not usually announce themselves as loyalty tests. They arrive as a leak investigation with a defined procedure, and the procedure does the work.

What history says: Purges of planning staffs before a war have a specific signature — the narrowing of the options paper. When planners learn that pessimism is a risk, the memo that reaches the President contains fewer branches. The 1965 and 2002 escalations both show staffs that had learned to stop offering the option they knew would be unwelcome.

What we’re watching next: Who is named acting director, and whether it is a career officer or an appointee. Whether the Justice Department confirms the referral on the record. Whether another Joint Staff or combatant-command officer is removed within a week. And whether the strike package narrows to options that need less planning staff — the quiet tell.

Sources: Reuters, CBS News, The New York Times, The Guardian, Al Jazeera, Oct 29

The Pentagon now calls the freezer failures at its own grocery stores a cyberattack

What happened: Sometime in late August, freezers at US military commissaries — at least six bases — started failing. Cooling units were not broken; in at least one case they were cranked up overnight, so the food was wilted and melted by morning. Fort Huachuca’s own Facebook page said the freezers had been put into ‘defrost mode’. A Nellis post called the problem system-wide. On Wednesday Reuters reported that the Pentagon is now treating the episode as a suspected cyberattack, and that US investigators have been in contact with their French counterparts over the attack that cooked the refrigeration in several hundred French supermarkets on the night of October 11-12.

Why it matters: Nobody has publicly said who did it. What is public is the shape: hundreds of independent businesses, or in this case a hundred-odd base grocery stores, all wired to the same vendor’s cloud so a distant technician can adjust a setpoint. One command, and every freezer on the network obeys at once. The French case destroyed tens of millions of euros of food in a day and set some units on fire. The American one was discovered not by an alert but by a shopper noticing soft ice cream, and it took roughly two months for anyone to call it an intrusion.

The pattern: This is the second time in a month that refrigeration — a boring, outsourced, remotely-managed utility — has been used as a way into a country’s supply chain. The military’s answer, announced on September 30, is called FORTRESS America: every major installation should be able to run for 90 days without the civilian power grid, water, fuel or communications, using microgrids, batteries and small reactors. So in the same fortnight the Pentagon begins to wall its bases off from the civilian world, it discovers its own grocery chain is sitting on it.

What history says: The 2021 Colonial Pipeline and JBS meat attacks taught the same lesson and were filed as one-offs: a single vendor, a single password, a single choke point, and a country’s fuel or beef stops. Nothing structural changed afterwards, because each incident could be described as a mistake by a careless company rather than a property of the architecture. The French and American freezers are the first case where the same seam was hit on two continents within weeks, which makes ‘careless company’ harder to sustain.

What we’re watching next: Whether the Pentagon or the FBI names an actor, and whether that naming comes from a cyber agency or from a podium. Whether the number of affected commissaries grows past six. And the real test: whether a completely different kind of remote-controlled system — a water utility, a payment network, a building’s heating — fails the same way before January, and gets reported as bad luck again.

Sources: Reuters, Oct 29 · Reuters / NBC News · Franceinfo and CNBC, Oct 13 · BBC, Oct 13

Hegseth fires the Pentagon’s top military planner, five days before a possible strike

What happened: Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth removed Army Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia as director for strategy, plans and policy on the Joint Chiefs of Staff — the officer who runs military planning for the Pentagon — on Tuesday. An official said the cause was leaks to the news media. The removal came the same week the President is weighing a fresh campaign against Iran.

Why it matters: The office that writes the plans, and with them the estimates of cost, duration and risk, is empty at the moment the decision is taken. That does not decide the outcome. It does mean the paper trail of who was consulted, and who warned against, will be thinner than it would otherwise have been.

The pattern: Sylvia is the latest in a long series. Chaplains, military lawyers, the Guard’s legal review, the officer overseeing a disputed promotion — the removals have moved steadily up and inward all year. What is new is that the purge has reached the shop that turns a political instruction into an operational plan, in the week the instruction may be given.

What history says: Civilian leaders have always reshuffled war staffs, and sometimes for good reason. The cleaner comparison is 2002-03: as the Iraq plans took shape, the officers who had testified about troop requirements were sidelined, and the estimates that survived were the ones that fit the calendar. The lesson historians draw is not that dissenters were right about everything — it is that once the estimating office is quiet, nobody can later show what was known.

What we’re watching next: Who fills the job, and whether the seat is still empty when a strike is ordered. Whether more removals follow inside the same directorate. Whether any retired officer or congressional committee puts the vacancy on the record next to the strike decision.

Sources: AP, CBS News, CNBC, Oct 28-29

Two tracks, both running in public: strikes after the vote, and a letter to Tehran

What happened: US officials have told several Middle East governments and regional allies to be prepared for possible renewed military action against Iran after the November 3 midterms, and for Iranian retaliation. The same day, the Wall Street Journal reported — and the President confirmed — that Vice President JD Vance had written to Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, to restart negotiations. Trump described the letter as being about offering medical supplies, said talks are running through mediators, and added that he does not know whether they will work. Oil fell: Brent under $114 a barrel, still up 10 percent on the month and the highest since April.

Why it matters: A peace track and a bombing track are now both official, both briefed to the press, and both scheduled around the same date. Markets read the letter and took risk out of the price. The Gulf governments read the briefings and were told to prepare for the opposite. Whichever happens, the administration will be able to say it had tried.

The pattern: This board has tracked the sequencing question for a month: whether the campaign against Iran was planned before or after the election. The answer keeps arriving in the same form — a diplomatic channel that produces communication but no mechanism, alongside a force posture that is never stood down. Rezaei is a hard-liner, not a negotiator, which fits a face-saving pause as well as it fits a deal.

What history says: Dual-track diplomacy is a normal instrument, and it usually looks exactly like this from outside — letters and carriers at the same time. The tell that distinguishes a real track from an exit ramp is procedural, not rhetorical: a named monitor, a mandate, a signed sheet, someone with the authority to inspect something. October has produced statements of willingness and no mechanism.

What we’re watching next: Whether a named monitoring arrangement exists before the vote. Whether the 96-hour readiness posture is relaxed. Whether the Telegraph’s report that Tehran is preparing to disrupt the midterms gets formally endorsed by an agency — the mirror-image claim to the strike warnings, filed five days out.

Sources: AP, WSJ, Irish Times/Bloomberg, Axios, The Telegraph, Al Jazeera, Oct 28-29

The Justice Department says FBI and ICE agents will not be at the polls

What happened: Late on Wednesday the Justice Department said that agents and employees of the FBI and Immigration and Customs Enforcement will not serve as monitors during next week’s elections. Earlier reporting had suggested the administration intended to send federal agents to polling places; the NAACP sued over voter intimidation.

Why it matters: This is a climb-down, and it is the second this month on the same ground: the courts have now refused the administration’s requests on ballot setting-aside, on mail screening, and on a special master. Polling places stay civilian-run.

The pattern: The administration has consistently advanced its election claims through litigation rather than through administration, and has lost at nearly every gate. What replaces the courtroom in the final week is not a new legal theory but a different venue — county parties, state prosecutors, and the President’s own microphone.

What history says: Federal monitoring at polls has a real and troubled history, from the 1965 Voting Rights Act’s observers to the 1981 New Jersey consent decree that constrained Republican ‘ballot security’ operations for 36 years. Every attempt to expand it has run into the same question — who watches the watchers — and the same answer, which is a court order.

What we’re watching next: Whether any other federal body appears at polling places in a different capacity. Whether the NAACP suit is withdrawn or pressed for a permanent order. And whether the retreat holds through election day, when the pressure is highest.

Sources: Reuters, Oct 28-29

Alaska’s governor opens a criminal investigation into the Peltola campaign

What happened: Governor Mike Dunleavy ordered a state investigation on Wednesday into whether Mary Peltola’s Senate campaign broke Alaska law by helping recruit a second candidate named Daniel J. Sullivan into the race — an allegation aired in a New York Times story, not by any election official. The President has said Peltola should be thrown off the ballot. The underlying question of whether the second Sullivan belongs on the ballot was decided by the Alaska Supreme Court in June, and the federal suit arrived after Alaskans had already started voting.

Why it matters: Five days before the vote, in a race that decides Senate control, the Democratic nominee is now the subject of a criminal inquiry opened by a Republican governor, on the basis of a newspaper article, about a matter a court has already ruled on.

The pattern: Three tracks are now running at once against the same candidate: a federal civil suit over ballot composition, a federal criminal referral against a sitting congresswoman in a different state, and a state criminal probe. This board has watched the shift all year from arguing a case to converting it — moving a question that was adjudicated and lost into a venue with different rules. A prosecutor sets the timetable, and the timetable is the election.

What history says: Same-name candidates are an old and mostly legal trick; ‘ballot composition’ schemes have been litigated since the nineteenth century. What is new is the escalation ladder: a lost civil ruling is normally the end of it. Turning a settled ballot question into a criminal referral days before an election is the kind of move that, in other countries, election observers would flag as the apparatus being used against an opponent.

What we’re watching next: Whether the probe announces findings before Nov 3 or conveniently after. Which charge, if any, is chosen — the charge reveals the theory. Whether the federal suit converts to a criminal referral. And whether the President repeats the demand for removal this week.

Sources: NYT, BBC, Washington Times, Forbes, Oct 28-29

Texas county Republicans sue to block certification unless every ballot is hand-counted

What happened: Republican county parties in Texas have sued to block certification of their own counties’ results unless every ballot is counted by hand. One suit, in Potter County, cites ‘AI threats’ as its grievance. The Texas Tribune reports that local GOP groups are preparing more.

Why it matters: This is the certification fight from the bottom up. Until now the pressure came from Washington — the Justice Department suing states. County parties need no permission, and they have something the federal agency never had: standing, as local parties entitled to observe their own counts.

The pattern: Hand-counting every ballot in a general election is not physically possible in the time the law allows, which is the point. The demand cannot be met, so the count cannot be certified on time, and the failure to certify becomes the evidence of the fraud. Meanwhile the people filing these suits are often the same people who sit on the boards that certify.

What history says: In 2022 a rural Nevada county tried a full hand count, missed the deadline, and had to be rescued by the state Supreme Court. In 2020 and 2022, county-level refusal to certify was the one tactic that came closest to working — not because it changed results, but because it ran out the clock and forced courts to act under time pressure.

What we’re watching next: How many counties file before Tuesday, and in which court. Whether any canvassing board actually refuses. Whether Texas state officials step in against the county parties — and how quickly.

Sources: Democracy Docket, Texas Tribune, Oct 28-29

A fifth drone attack sinks a container ship off Bulgaria — and Sofia declines to call it a NATO matter

What happened: Three maritime drones struck the Greek-managed container ship Contship Jet about 21 nautical miles off Bulgaria’s coast on Wednesday. The ship sank; all 18 crew are safe in Varna. It is the fifth drone attack on shipping in Bulgaria’s exclusive economic zone this month. Bulgaria’s defence minister says he assumes the drones are Russian, reasoning that it would be illogical for Ukraine to attack its own ships. The Prime Minister summoned the Russian ambassador and called it a gross violation of international law — and ruled out invoking NATO’s Article 4 or 5. Opposition parties are demanding the consultations anyway, and Bulgaria has asked NATO for fighter jets and counter-drone systems.

Why it matters: The BBC’s own note is the important one: a few miles further west and this would have been Article 5 territory — an armed attack on a member of the alliance that all members are bound to answer. Instead it is shipping risk. Over ten days, five-plus cargo ships have been hit off Romania and Bulgaria and at least 23 sailors have been killed.

The pattern: Attacks accumulate below the threshold of response, week by week, until the new level of violence is simply the weather. The constraint is not the attacker’s restraint. It is the willingness of a member government to name what is happening, and Sofia has decided not to.

What history says: This is how the pre-war years of the last century ran: incidents that were each deniable, each answered with a protest, until the accumulated record was unmistakable and too late to shape. The Corfu incident of 1923, the successive Baltic and Black Sea ‘incidents’ of 1938-39 — the pattern is a decision, repeated weekly, not to escalate.

What we’re watching next: Whether the NATO request produces a permanent counter-drone contribution or only aircraft and statements. Whether Romania, with a much longer coastline and its own incidents, breaks ranks and calls for Article 4. And whether the cadence holds into next week — five in a month suggests it will.

Sources: Reuters, AP, BBC, BTA, Kyiv Post, Oct 28-29

The White House’s closing argument is a video of France on fire

What happened: The White House posted a video of violent French protest scenes on Wednesday with a message: this is the future you want? A vote for the Democrats, it said, is a vote for ‘chaos, looting and destruction’. Senators and surrogates repeated the line — France is a preview of what the American left would do here. The French Senate, meanwhile, has backed a bill requiring rioters to pay for the damage from unrest that has cost more than a billion euros.

Why it matters: With five days left and polls pointing the wrong way, the closing message is not inflation, not the war, not the cost of groceries. It is fear of disorder, sourced from another country.

The pattern: This board flagged the French riots being imported into the American argument ten days ago. The import works because the images need no translation: burning cars, police lines, a European city. Nothing in the ad is false about the footage. Everything about the causal claim is unstated, which is what makes it effective — the viewer supplies the reason the cities are burning.

What history says: Closing on disorder is the oldest move in the book — 1968, 1988’s Willie Horton, 2020’s ‘Biden’s America’. It works when the incumbent can plausibly promise order and the challenger cannot. It tends to fail when the disorder is happening on the incumbent’s watch, or when the economy is the thing voters actually feel. This year both are true at once.

What we’re watching next: Whether the France frame displaces the economic message entirely in the last four days — the ads are the tell, not the speeches. Whether French officials respond, which would give the story a second life. And whether the same footage appears in Senate races where the Democrat is a moderate.

Sources: White House post, Oct 28 · The Hill, Times of India, Fox News, Oct 29

The Fed holds rates with two votes to raise them, into a hundred-dollar oil war

What happened: The Federal Reserve left rates at 3.75 to 4 percent on Wednesday, in a 10-2 vote. Both dissents — Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack — wanted rates higher. Inflation is running at 3.7 percent, and the Fed raised a quarter point last month. Brent crude is near $114 and West Texas Intermediate near $101. Shell’s profits doubled to $10.8 billion. The war against Iran has cost an estimated $47.1 billion so far.

Why it matters: The central bank is debating whether to tighten into a war-driven oil shock, five days before an election, with inflation already above target and growth tracking around 3.4 percent. That combination — decent growth, high inflation, expensive energy, rising rates — is the one configuration that leaves a government with no comfortable lever.

The pattern: War, energy, and prices have become one system. Each escalation in the Gulf moves the barrel; each move in the barrel moves the inflation number; each inflation number moves the rate decision; each rate decision lands on mortgages and car loans in Ohio and Michigan. The electorate experiences foreign policy as a grocery bill, which is exactly why the sequencing of the strike matters so much politically.

What history says: 1973-74 and 1979 both produced this trap: an oil shock, an inflation problem, and a central bank that tightened into a recession. The difference now is that the oil is not being embargoed by producers — it is being priced for a war that has not yet resumed. Markets are charging a premium for a decision that has been briefed to allies but not taken.

What we’re watching next: December: traders expect a hike, and two members already voted for one. Whether a post-election strike pushes the barrel through $120, and whether that forces the Fed’s hand. And whether the war’s price tag becomes an argument in the closing days — $47.1 billion is a number nobody has been asked to defend.

Sources: CNBC, CNN, Reuters, Irish Times/Bloomberg, PBS, Oct 28-29
