<summary>Explainer: what a "color revolution" is — and why it's suddenly everywhere on the right</summary>

<p><b>What happened:</b> In the past two days, former national security adviser Michael Flynn called for the arrest of former officials who took part in a nonprofit planning exercise, calling them "traitors." The material fueling the push traces to Jennica Pounds — the online figure "DataRepublican" — who has described what she calls "color revolution" tactics inside the United States. Pounds is not just an online commentator: the Defense Department confirmed she has held a special-government-employee post since July. Heritage Foundation's oversight project director Mike Howell described the country as drifting toward a "cold civil war," and Senator Mike Lee amplified the claims while saying he hoped the recordings were a deepfake. Thirteen days before the election, the language of foreign-backed subversion is being pointed at American civic groups and former officials.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters:</b> "Color revolution" is a real term of art from the 2000s, naming popular uprisings — Serbia's bulldozer revolution, Georgia's Rose revolution, Ukraine's Orange revolution — in which mass civic movements, often backed by Western funding and training, toppled entrenched governments. Authoritarian governments from Moscow to Tehran have long used the phrase as a catch-all accusation: any domestic protest, they claim, is not real grievance but a foreign-directed script. That same vocabulary is now being applied inside the United States — with the accusers in power.</p>

<p><b>The pattern:</b> The language does specific work. First, it converts citizens into agents: a protest is not people angry about something, it is the opening move of a foreign-style plot. Second, it converts preparation into conspiracy: the targeted materials here were scenario exercises — groups game-planning what would happen if an election were contested or court orders ignored — and planning for a constitutional crisis is being recast as plotting one. Third, it prepares arrests before any crime: calling people "traitors" who should be "found and arrested" pre-frames future prosecutions as counterintelligence, not politics. And there is an inversion at the center: the leaked "illegitimacy criteria" document at issue defines an election as illegitimate if, say, a government defies court orders or turns force on voters — conditions that describe the administration's own documented conduct more closely than anyone else's.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> Accusing the opposition of foreign-directed subversion is the oldest move in the authoritarian playbook — the Reichstag fire was blamed on communists within weeks of the 1933 emergency decree; decades of crackdowns followed the same script. What is newer is the pipeline: a government-adjacent researcher's claims flow through political figures with institutional standing (a senator, a former national security adviser, a foundation's oversight arm) and emerge sounding like an official finding. That laundering — fringe claim to authoritative voice — is how pre-justification is built before any enforcement happens.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Four tells. Whether the Justice Department opens any investigation into the named groups or former officials — that would turn the rhetoric into an instrument. Whether "traitor" language migrates from personal social media into White House or DOJ official channels. Whether the administration adopts the "color revolution" frame itself to describe domestic opposition. And whether Pounds's government post is used to present her material as government-sourced. If the recordings at the center of this turn out to be edited or fabricated, the story changes — but the push happened either way.</p>
