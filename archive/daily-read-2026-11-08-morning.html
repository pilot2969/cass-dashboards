[10:40] The president replaced his chief of staff with the man who built the deportation machine

Three days after losing both houses of Congress, the president changed the person who controls access to him — and gave the job to the official who ran his immigration crackdown.

What happened. On Friday afternoon Donald Trump removed Susie Wiles as White House chief of staff. She had held the job longer than anyone else in his term. Her replacement is Stephen Miller, who was already deputy chief of staff and homeland security adviser, and who is the author of the administration’s mass-deportation programme. The change was announced by the president himself, days after Republicans lost control of both the House and the Senate.

Why it matters. After a defeat, organisations usually widen. They bring in the people who were kept at a distance, they soften the edges, they look for what the voters were trying to say. This administration did the opposite. The most important job in the building — the one that decides who gets to see the president and which arguments reach him — went to the official whose agenda has the fewest legal obstacles in front of it. That is not disarray. Disarray moves people around at random. This moved authority in one direction, and the direction was toward enforcement.

The pattern. When a leader’s coalition shrinks, the circle around the leader usually shrinks faster than the coalition does. The people who remain are the ones who never flinched, and the ones who flinched are already gone. The result looks like loyalty from the outside and feels like narrowing from the inside. It also changes what reaches the desk: a small circle with an enforcement portfolio tends to hear enforcement arguments, because nobody is left in the room whose job is to say the courts will not allow it.

What history says. In November 1932 the Nazi party lost 34 seats in the German parliament. Its response was not moderation. Within five weeks Gregor Strasser, the party’s own internal alternative, had resigned, and authority had concentrated in the leader’s office. The chancellorship followed eight weeks later. In 1973, as the Watergate investigation closed in, Richard Nixon narrowed his circle to his two most loyal aides and then fired the special prosecutor; that arrangement survived roughly a year before the removal mechanism finished the job. The pattern is not that hardliners always win. It is that a leader under pressure almost never responds by widening the room, and the narrowing is the thing to watch, not the rhetoric.

What we’re watching next. Whether the institutional staff start leaving. The White House counsel, the legislative affairs director, the budget director — those are the jobs held by people whose function is to tell the president what he cannot do. If they go in the next month, the circle is closing and the constraint is going with them. The second test is behaviour rather than personnel: whether deportations, workplace raids and denaturalisation cases increase without any new law being passed. A crackdown that needs a statute is politics. A crackdown that does not is something else.

Sources: New York Times, NBC News, Washington Post, The Guardian

[10:34] Both sides are now saying the other side’s attacks did not happen

Explosions were heard across southern Iran on Saturday night. Tehran suggested they were its own military at work. Washington called its own strikes “minor.” Both sides are still reporting ships on fire.

What happened. On Saturday evening residents across southern Iran reported blasts — on Qeshm island, at Sirik, Bandar Abbas and Jask, all near the Strait of Hormuz. A semi-official Iranian news agency said two missiles had hit Qeshm. Then Iranian state television said officials had confirmed no missile strikes on Iranian territory at all, and suggested the explosions were connected to Iranian military activity in the waterway. A day earlier the president had confirmed American strikes on Iranian air-defence equipment, describing them as “minor”: “they built a little bit of an anti-aircraft device and we knocked it out in about 15 minutes, maybe five.” At sea, Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said two oil tankers hit mines and a third caught fire in the strait; the United States said its aircraft disabled a ship in the Gulf of Oman; a British maritime monitoring body logged an incident east of the UAE and could not say what caused it. Nobody has confirmed anybody’s account. On Sunday, Saudi Arabia said a building belonging to its state oil company in Riyadh had been damaged by falling debris after an attempted attack.

Why it matters. The important thing is not how many explosions there were. It is that both sides are describing the same violent events as something other than the other side’s attack. That is a choice, made twice, in the same week. Ambiguity of this kind is useful: it lets two governments keep hitting each other while each denies that a decision has been taken. It is what a country does when it wants the damage without the announcement.

The pattern. Most long conflicts eventually develop a zone where violence is real but unattributed — a ship is mined, a pipeline is blown up, a base is rocketed, and everyone understands who did it while no government says so. The zone exists because the alternative is a decision, and a decision has consequences at home. The 2019 attacks on tankers in the same waters, and the strike on Saudi Arabia’s Abqaiq processing plant that autumn, followed exactly this logic: the capability was demonstrated, the attribution was withheld, and no response was required. The current war appears to be moving into that zone from above, which is a strange direction to travel — normally you leave ambiguity behind when you start bombing.

What history says. In 1987 and 1988 the same waterway hosted a shooting war that neither side called one. Kuwait’s tankers were reflagged under the American flag, the US Navy escorted them, both sides struck shipping, and the whole thing ran for more than a year with each attack explained as something else — a mine of unknown origin, a defensive measure, an accident. It ended through a negotiated truce, not a decisive battle, and it killed hundreds of sailors who were mostly not soldiers. The lesson is not that ambiguity keeps wars small. It is that ambiguity lets wars run a long time without anyone having to justify them.

What we’re watching next. Whether anyone confirms anything. If a strike is acknowledged by both governments in the next ten days, the war is still being conducted with announcements and the escalation is measurable. If it is not — if the pattern becomes a series of incidents nobody owns — then the fighting can intensify while every public indicator reads calm, and the thing to watch becomes insurance: the premiums shipowners pay to move oil through the Gulf, and the number of hulls actually lost. Those numbers are set by people with money at risk, and they do not care what either government says.

Sources: CNBC, Al Jazeera, NBC News, The Guardian

[10:28] Nine people died in Kraków. Poland now has to decide what he was

A man opened fire from a car in Kraków’s main square on Saturday, killing six. Police then found three more bodies at his home. He held far-right views. What Poland does next matters more than what he believed.

What happened. On Saturday afternoon a gunman drove into the main square of Kraków, in southern Poland, and opened fire with an AR-15-style rifle at people in a crowded market square. Six people were killed — four women and two men, one of them a British citizen — and sixteen were injured. Police shot and wounded him. When counter-terrorism officers searched his home in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski, about 140km away, they found three more bodies: two women and a man, reported as members of his family. Poland’s defence minister called the attack an act of terror, and the country declared a day of national mourning for Sunday. Authorities describe the suspect as a Polish man with ultranationalist, far-right views.

Why it matters. One man with a rifle is not a movement, and the number of dead tells you nothing about how many people share his politics. This distinction gets lost immediately after attacks like this one, in both directions: those who want the ideology treated as the cause, and those who want it treated as irrelevant. The honest position is that the attack establishes availability and not strength — the vocabulary is in circulation, and one person can act on it without a network, a leader or a plan. That is a real finding about a society. It is not a measurement of an organisation.

The pattern. The question that follows an attack like this is not what the gunman believed. It is which machinery the state reaches for. There are two doors. Through the first, the act is processed as a crime: a man is charged, his politics are the motive, and the trial is the response. Through the second, the act is processed as security: emergency legislation, expanded powers for the security services, a formal category for the movement, and a permanent budget line. Which door a state opens depends less on the attack than on whether the door is already unlocked.

What history says. Norway in 2011 and New Zealand in 2019 are the clearest comparison, because the death tolls were wildly different and the responses were the opposite of what the tolls would predict. Anders Breivik killed 77 people and Norway answered with a trial — the political response was an insistence on openness, and no crackdown on the milieu he claimed to serve. New Zealand had 51 killed in Christchurch and changed its gun laws in under four weeks. What differed was not the severity of the attack. It was whether the government had a legislative route open and a public ready to accept it. In Germany, the Halle and Hanau attacks of 2019 and 2020 were followed within a year by gun-law changes and a formal shift of domestic intelligence resources toward right-wing extremism. Same continent, same decade, different doors.

What we’re watching next. Poland is a particular case, because its government is already in a standoff with its own constitutional court over whether the judiciary is legitimate. A state in that position finds emergency powers unusually easy to justify — the ordinary machinery is already being described as broken. So the thing to watch is not whether Poland passes something. It is what kind of something: a security law aimed at a movement, or a security law aimed at a problem, and whether the court standoff pauses while the country mourns or gets folded into the answer.

Sources: AP, CNN, BBC, The Guardian

[10:22] The $3 trillion came back a little, which is not the same as coming back

Technology stocks bounced on Friday after their worst day since 2020. The Dow fell. Two days into the test, the money is moving around inside the trade rather than returning to it.

What happened. Thursday was the rout: the Nasdaq fell 5.5%, the S&P 4.4%, the Dow more than 1,800 points, and the Philadelphia semiconductor index lost more than 12% in a single session — the worst day for chip stocks since April 2025. Roughly $3 trillion of market value went with it, after voters in ten states approved restrictions on data centres in almost every contest where they appeared. Friday was calmer: the Nasdaq rose 1.1%, the S&P 0.3%, and small companies 0.9%, while the Dow slipped 0.5%. For the week, technology had its worst run since April 2025. SpaceX shares rose almost 7% on regulatory news.

Why it matters. A bounce is not a recovery, and the composition matters more than the index. If the money were returning to the same trade, the whole complex would rise together. Instead the Dow fell while the Nasdaq rose, which means money moved from one part of the market to another rather than coming back in. The market is still deciding whether forty-one local zoning votes are a permanent change in the cost of building artificial intelligence, or a scare it can buy through.

The pattern. When a boom’s constraint changes from money to permission, the first repricing looks like a panic and the second looks like a business decision. Prices move in a day; building plans move in a quarter. Between those two events there is usually a stretch of weeks where the tape oscillates and executives say reassuring things, and the only real evidence is what companies do with their capital budgets rather than what they say on television.

What history says. The same sequence ran through the nuclear construction boom of the 1970s, which collapsed not because demand fell but because local opposition and licensing delays pushed construction times and costs past what utilities could finance. It took about four years between the first cancelled plant and the end of new orders, and share prices gave almost no advance warning — the losses showed up in project cancellations and write-downs, quarter by quarter. The tell was never the index. It was the utility that walked away from a site and explained why.

What we’re watching next. Whether the money returns within five trading sessions — Wednesday is the deadline we set — and whether any large technology company cuts capital spending and names permits as the reason. That second event is the one that would convert a price move into a plan. Until it happens, this remains a market story that has not yet become an industry story.

Sources: CNBC, TheStreet, Yahoo Finance
