[10:05] The defense secretary put the troops between himself and the vote

He did not argue the charges were wrong. He argued that convicting him convicts the soldiers who obeyed him.

What happened. The defence secretary held a press conference this morning on the eight impeachment articles filed against him on Monday. He said: “Put the Secretary on trial today and the men and women that carried out those orders tomorrow — if they are going to come for those warriors, they have to come through me.” He closed with the enlistment oath’s own phrase: “we will fight all enemies, foreign and domestic.” The resolution was filed as a privileged measure, which forces the House to act on it within two legislative days.

Why it matters. The charges — the Iran operation, the seizure of a foreign leader, detention orders, the restriction of a senator’s speech — are no longer the subject. The subject is now whether a member of Congress is for the troops. That is not a defence of conduct; it is a change of question. And it costs him something real: he is conceding that what happened happened, and that responsibility runs down the chain of command. That is a genuine constitutional argument, deployed to make the vote harder to cast.

The pattern. This is how accountability is converted into loyalty. Make the ordinary exercise of oversight look like an attack on the people who serve, and the vote stops being about evidence and becomes about whose side you are on. The tell is that the defence never cites the allegations. It cites the accused’s subordinates.

What history says. Officials facing removal have used the same move for two centuries: convert a personal inquiry into an institutional one. Secretary of War Belknap resigned in 1876 rather than let the House vote, because the Senate would not convict a man who had already gone. President Nixon’s defenders argued for two years that pursuing him would damage the presidency, not that the acts were lawful. The argument that a prosecution of one official would break the institution is always available, and it is always available precisely when the evidence is strongest.

What we’re watching next. Whether the President repeats the framing in his own voice. If he does, protection becomes doctrine and the secretary stays. If he does not, this was a man defending his own job. And whether a serving or retired officer says publicly: we carried out lawful orders, do not use us. That is the one answer the secretary has no reply to.

Sources: The New York Times, Roll Call, BBC News, CBS News

[10:00] Seven in ten Americans want Congress to end the Iran war. He is arguing with that.

The policy the impeachment articles object to is the policy most voters want reversed.

What happened. A four-day Reuters/Ipsos poll completed on Monday found 71% of Americans want the new Democratic-majority Congress to pass legislation ending the war with Iran — 93% of Democrats and 51% of Republicans. Majorities also want tariffs repealed. The poll was published on the morning of the defence secretary’s press conference.

Why it matters. The war is not a fringe objection being raised by one Kentucky congressman. It is the majority position, including among the President’s own voters. That changes what the House vote is about: not whether the war was legal, but whether the people who voted for change get it. It also changes the cost of the loyalty argument. Members are being asked to protect a secretary over a policy their constituents want ended.

The pattern. When a government’s signature policy loses the electorate and the institution, the fight moves to procedure. The privileged resolution, the recess appointment, the emergency appeal — all ways of keeping a decision inside a body that has not yet voted. Note that the articles and the poll are about the same thing, and only one of them can be ignored.

What history says. Public opinion did not end the Vietnam war; the appropriations calendar did. It did not end the Iraq war; a status-of-forces deadline and a new majority did. Polling creates permission, never action. The mechanism that ends a war is always a vote that stops paying for it — which is why a war-powers fight is a fight about procedure.

What we’re watching next. Whether any member offers a war-powers resolution of their own alongside the impeachment vote. An impeachment of one man can be survived. A vote on funding cannot be avoided indefinitely.

Sources: Reuters via US News, Al-Monitor, The Independent

[09:55] The Supreme Court let the wall through Big Bend — again on the clock, not the merits

Fourth time this autumn the Court has given the government the time rather than the ruling.

What happened. The Supreme Court on Monday lifted a lower court’s order that had halted border wall construction in the Big Bend region of southwest Texas, including inside Big Bend National Park. The administration had asked for emergency relief. The order allows construction to resume while litigation continues. Environmental groups, the Lipan Apache tribe and local ranchers had challenged the project.

Why it matters. Nothing in the order says the wall is lawful. It says the building can continue until a court says otherwise — which, given the pace of the underlying litigation, means the wall will largely exist before its legality is decided. Emergency relief has become the routine instrument of the administration’s domestic programme, and it is being granted with the same consistency as in the election-integrity cases.

The pattern. A court that cannot decide quickly can still decide who is permitted to act. When the interim posture consistently favours one party, the interim posture is the outcome. The merits become a review of a fait accompli.

What history says. The border wall’s first section went up in 2008 under a 2005 waiver authority that let the executive suspend environmental law for the fence. Seventeen years later the same region is back, and the same instrument is being used: build now, litigate later. In the 1930s the Supreme Court struck down the early New Deal on emergency-relief grounds and then reversed itself within two years, after the statutes had reshaped the country in the meantime.

What we’re watching next. Whether the district court’s merits ruling arrives before large stretches are built. And whether the government waives environmental review for the park section, as it did in 2008 — that waiver would be a much clearer statement than this order.

Sources: AP News, Al Jazeera, The Guardian, Politico

[09:50] The Justice Department’s litigation chief quit, and the resignations are stacking up

The man who runs the government’s courtroom defences is the third Senate-confirmed official out since the election.

What happened. Assistant Attorney General Brett Shumate, who heads the Justice Department’s civil division, resigned on Monday. The attorney general announced it publicly. The civil division is the office that defends the government in court — the injunctions, the wall, the enforcement challenges, and most of what a new Congress will litigate. The civil rights division head resigned the previous week, and other senior officials have gone since the midterms.

Why it matters. This is a capacity story before it is an ideological one. The civil division is where the administration’s programmes are actually defended, and it is being handed over in the middle of the largest litigation load in years, with a new Congress arriving. When the people who know how the defences work leave, the government loses cases by default.

The pattern. Post-defeat departures are normal. What is not normal is the concentration in the litigation and civil-rights divisions — the two places where a departing administration’s programme is most exposed to legal challenge. People leave where the work becomes indefensible, and they leave before they have to sign it.

What history says. The Saturday Night Massacre of 1973 was three resignations in one evening, and it was a firing, not a walkout. The cleaner parallel is 2007, when nine US attorneys were dismissed and the department’s credibility took years to recover — the resignations were the story, but the damage was the institutional memory. A division that loses its leadership in a single week does not lose one week of work. It loses the four years of cases it was carrying.

What we’re watching next. Who replaces him, and whether the replacement is a career litigator or a loyalist. A career lawyer means the defences continue on their own. A loyalist means the defences are being chosen rather than argued — and that decisions currently made in court will start being made in the department.

Sources: ABC News, Just The News, NPR
