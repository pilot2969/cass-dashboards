<summary>[EVENING] Justice Department charges 28 people with voting crimes — two weeks before the election</summary>

<p><b>What happened:</b> On Monday the Justice Department announced criminal charges against 28 foreign nationals living in the US, accusing them of voter fraud in California. It comes three days after the department sued California to stop counting roughly 20,000 mail ballots until each voter's citizenship can be verified — a check election officials say cannot be completed in time. The campaign has a name: "Operation Protect the Vote."</p>

<p><b>Why it matters:</b> The charges are small in number but large in position: they arrive <i>before</i> the election, not after. A fraud story needs examples, and now it has them — filed, press-released, and quoted, two weeks before votes are counted.</p>

<p><b>The pattern:</b> First the polls were called rigged. Now the government itself is producing the exhibits for the claim that the count is rigged. Reporters noted some of the 28 appear to have been confused about whether they were eligible at all — but for the story's purpose, an arrest photo matters more than a conviction.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> Governments that planned to contest an election's result have always needed a violation to point to. The exhibit is staged in advance so that the losing side's grievance arrives pre-documented, regardless of what the tally shows.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether these 28 charges show up in ads, rallies or official statements before Nov 3 — that's the handoff from courtroom to campaign. And how courts rule on the ballot-blocking request: a refusal itself becomes exhibit number twenty-nine.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> Wars in chokepoints historically end not when one side quits but when the world finds another route. Until one exists, each passing day raises the cost for everyone — and the government holding the war's calendar decides whose cost counts.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Any attack on an American-flagged ship. So far the line has held — the moment it doesn't, expect the war to stop being background noise.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> In the 1930s, the erosion of legislative power in Germany didn't happen through a dramatic seizure. It happened through exactly this kind of step: each one defensible in isolation, each one met with silence, until the legislature met less and mattered less. The test is never one decree. It's whether the body that wrote the law fights back when it's undone.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether this same self-certification trick gets reused on a different issue — any deadline, funding rule, or restriction this administration dislikes. If it happens a second time, we'll know it wasn't a one-off; it's a tool.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> Declining empires have always kept allies in line this way — not with treaties but with dependence, and the unspoken promise that dependence can end. When Britain couldn't hold the Suez Canal in 1956, its allies stopped waiting for London. Today's Ukraine knows which switch controls its battlefield visibility, and it isn't in Kyiv.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> This is the "stab in the back" pattern: the loss is pre-explained before it happens, so the defeat itself becomes evidence of theft rather than of arithmetic.
