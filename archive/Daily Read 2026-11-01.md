<summary>A Minnesota police chief unplugs Flock's cameras himself — because the company never acknowledged the cancellation</summary>

<p><b>What happened:</b> Plymouth, Minnesota's city council voted unanimously to cancel its Flock Safety contract. More than a week later the company had not responded, and the cameras were still mounted and still powered. Police Chief Eric Fadden directed city staff to physically disconnect the power cords so the cameras could not function or record plates. Sixteen permanent cameras, per local reporting. The Drive's Joel Feder, who lives in Plymouth, checked two of them and found both dead. KARE 11 found the same pattern in several other Minnesota cities that voted to remove their cameras: the cameras are still on the poles.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters:</b> This is the third city in three months where a cancellation did not stop the cameras. In Dayton, Minnesota, the city told Flock on Sept 18 that the program was on hold and no work orders were to be completed; on Sept 29 Flock installed and activated two cameras anyway, then said removal would take 60 days. In Grass Valley, California, cameras came back after removal under an "automated maintenance system" that logged the removal itself as an equipment failure. Each case has its own procedural excuse and the same result.</p>

<p><b>The pattern:</b> The contract stopped being a control and became a request. The default state of the system is ON, so every administrative "off" has to be enforced against a machine that keeps generating installs. Sixty days of "removal window" is sixty days of live coverage, and an automated work-order queue does not read stop-work orders. The chokepoint moved from the vote to the power cable — which is why an afternoon with a pair of hands accomplished what a unanimous council vote could not.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> Enclosures are usually described at the point of entry — the vote, the contract, the sale. But the ones that hold are defended at the point of exit, which is why the enclosure of the commons ran through fences and gates rather than through Parliament, and why the early telegraph and railroad monopolies fought hardest over the right to string wire, not the right to charge for it. A property claim that cannot be switched off is not a licence, it is a tenancy.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether Plymouth's cameras stay dark or come back on under a maintenance order; whether other cities check whether their cancellation ever took effect; whether Flock responds publicly; and whether any state writes a physical-removal right into its ALPR statute, because that is the only version of this that closes.</p>

<summary>LA drops Flock for Axon plate readers — the largest swap yet, and the streets stay covered</summary>

<p><b>What happened:</b> After suspending its Flock contract in July, the LAPD moved forward this week with an Axon automated licence plate reader pilot, cleared by the City Council over objections from members who wanted a veto. The Los Angeles Times reports the transition as a vendor replacement, not a withdrawal. The Basin's mapping of the county counted 2,190 readers tagged Flock out of roughly 3,127 inside the 88 cities.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters:</b> This is the largest single jurisdiction in the country to swap rather than subtract, in the one market where the backlash is loudest. Local organizers said the quiet part directly: swapping one vendor for another does not fix a surveillance architecture. The visible object of the protest is removed and the coverage is not.</p>

<p><b>The pattern:</b> The brand absorbs the outrage. Flock is the only ALPR company most people can name, which makes it the natural target and the natural sacrifice — Axon's own CEO has asked for cameras redesigned so they do not look like Flock's. Resistance concentrates where it can see, and the substrate transfers to a lower-scrutiny skin. The same swap already happened in Longmont, Denver and Syracuse, where cities celebrated dropping Flock and re-installed Axon on the same streets.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> This is the standard shape of regulated-industry succession: the trust-busting era broke Standard Oil into pieces that reassembled as a set of smaller companies with the same shareholders, and Ma Bell's divestiture produced successors that reconvened inside a decade. The test of a reform is never which name leaves the market — it is whether the capacity leaves with it.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether the pilot becomes a contract; whether the county's Flock node count actually falls or is simply re-tagged; whether the inspector general's audit recommendations on retention and sharing get written into the Axon terms; and whether any city that dropped Flock can show a lower total reader count a year later.</p>

<summary>The war is rescheduled, not cancelled: "one or two sites", and Iran reopens the nuclear question</summary>

<p><b>What happened:</b> Rubio said Saturday the President has not decided on striking Iran and would prefer a deal. The same day, Trump said the US needs to take care of "one or two sites", after a call with Netanyahu about renewed strikes. Iran's security chief Rezaei told state television that Tehran is debating whether to keep its ban on nuclear weapons if hostilities resume — no decision taken, the price signal published. Qatari mediators met Iranian envoys in Doha again this weekend on a seven-day step-by-step plan.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters:</b> The no-strike-before-the-vote commitment is intact, but the negotiation has been reclassified from the alternative to war into one option of two. Two clocks are running: Doha's daily steps and a strike package waiting on a carrier that is not yet in theater.</p>

<p><b>The pattern:</b> A deadline that keeps moving without being cancelled is a commitment device, not a plan. Each restatement narrows the distance between "prefer a deal" and "one or two sites" while the audience is told nothing has been decided.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> The 1962 missile crisis ran on two clocks — the quarantine and the back channel — and the back channel is what ended it. What made 2003 different was that the military clock had no counterpart, so the diplomatic track could only ratify a decision already made. Which one this becomes depends on whether Doha produces something dated.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Any strike before Tuesday's close of polls; whether Doha produces something signable and when it is dated; whether Rezaei's remark survives 72 hours; whether the Roosevelt reaches CENTCOM before "one or two sites" becomes an order.</p>

<summary>The Ninth Circuit said no twice, and DOJ still hasn't asked the Supreme Court</summary>

<p><b>What happened:</b> The Ninth Circuit denied the Justice Department's bid to block counties from counting roughly 20,000 California mail ballots a second time this week — an amended order issued 30 October, 2-1. As of tonight there is still no application on the Supreme Court docket. Separately, DOJ is deploying about 1,000 poll observers for Tuesday, which the department is describing as the largest deployment in its history.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters:</b> The administration's sharpest pre-election legal instrument is stalled at the circuit level two days out. The observer deployment is unaffected and proceeding on schedule, so the visible machinery and the actual litigation have come apart: one is running, the other is blocked.</p>

<p><b>The pattern:</b> When a case keeps losing at the intermediate level and the emergency application doesn't get filed, the deadline is usually doing the work. An injunction granted Tuesday is worth more than one granted Friday, and a denial costs less if it never becomes a Supreme Court precedent.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> Bush v. Gore was filed and decided inside four days, in the other direction — an emergency application filed when the clock was nearly out. The variable isn't urgency; it's whether the filer believes the Court will take it. In 2000 they did. This week, with two circuit losses and no filing, the record suggests the filer has read the room.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether an application lands Monday, and how narrow the relief is — a segregation of those 20,000 ballots is a different claim from a statewide count freeze. Whether the observer deployment produces a documented challenge on Tuesday. Whether the circuit's amended order survives a motion to reconsider.</p>

<summary>Stevens endorses the Republican, two days after her party's unity event was cancelled</summary>

<p><b>What happened:</b> Michigan Representative Haley Stevens endorsed Republican Mike Rogers for Senate on Sunday afternoon, hours after Abdul El-Sayed's campaign asked her to stay away from a shared Detroit church event this morning — after which the two did not appear together at all. Her chief of staff resigned. The same afternoon, at a rally in Iowa, the President repeatedly called El-Sayed a "jihadist."</p>

<p><b>Why it matters:</b> A sitting Democratic member endorsing the Republican nominee in an open Senate race, in the final 48 hours, is a real defection rather than a messaging story. The resignation of the chief of staff says it was a decision made against the office's own institutional advice. And it lands in the same news cycle as a targeting frame on the Democratic nominee — one from inside the coalition, one from outside it.</p>

<p><b>The pattern:</b> Coalition fracture and external targeting arrive together, and each makes the other cheaper. A candidate framed as a foreign-policy liability by the White House is easier for a cross-pressured incumbent to abandon; an incumbent's abandonment is evidence for the frame.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> Late-cycle defections in Senate races are usually about the defector's own constituency, not the nominee's ideology — 1996 and 2016 both produced last-weekend endorsements across party lines by members whose districts had already moved. What makes this different is the timing relative to a named, official targeting frame. Watch whether any other member follows within 24 hours; a single defection is a person, three is a caucus.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether Rogers accepts the endorsement publicly before Tuesday. Whether other Michigan Democrats distance themselves. Whether the "jihadist" line appears in paid advertising or stays in rally transcripts — the difference between a message and a mood.</p>

<summary>"New weaponry" but no new decision — Iran moves its threat up a rung without moving its program</summary>

<p><b>What happened:</b> Iran's security council chief Mohsen Rezaei told state media today that Tehran has still reached no new decision on its ban on nuclear weapons, and added that Iran would use "new weaponry" in any response to attack. The Supreme Leader separately warned of a "geographically unrestricted response" if Iran is struck again. No technical indicator moved: no seismic event, no IAEA special report, no change in facility imagery.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters:</b> This is the clearest test case available for whether a nuclear threat means anything before it is a capability. The rhetoric has climbed from "we are debating the doctrine" to "new weaponry" in 48 hours while the observable programme has not changed at all. That gap is the measurement.</p>

<p><b>The pattern:</b> Declaratory escalation is cheap and reversible; technical escalation is expensive and detectable. States that intend to cross the threshold usually go quiet first. States that intend to be heard talking about it do the opposite.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> The 2017 North Korean sequence matched declaratory first, technical second — but the technical step was verified within weeks. Iran's own 2012 threats produced no test and no breakout. The base rate for a threat becoming a test inside a 48-hour window is close to zero, which is why the forecast held at 7% through the weekend.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether a principal — not Rezaei — repeats "new weaponry" before Tuesday. Whether the IAEA asks for access. Whether the Doha talks survive the escalation in language.</p>

<summary>Oil closes the pre-vote weekend high but unbroken — the shock that was supposed to arrive didn't</summary>

<p><b>What happened:</b> Brent settled around $113 a barrel and WTI around $102 on 1 November, both below Friday's four-year high of $116.72 and both far above where they sat a year ago. Over the final week there was no breach of the Strategic Petroleum Reserve's operating floor, no Bab el-Mandeb closure, and no new major convoy loss. OPEC+ held its existing production targets through December.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters:</b> One of the sharper pre-registered forecasts on the board — that a physical oil shock would break before the midterms and act as the regime's fatality — expires Tuesday with its physical leg untriggered. The price leg is real and large. The mechanism it was meant to ride on never fired.</p>

<p><b>The pattern:</b> A price can be high for a year without becoming an event. Forecasts that require a threshold to be crossed by a date fail most often not because the direction was wrong but because the crossing didn't happen on schedule — and the direction was, in fact, exactly right.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> 1973 and 1979 both produced price shocks that broke governments; 2008 produced a price shock that broke nothing politically because it arrived with a demand collapse rather than a supply one. The question after Tuesday is whether $113 with the reserve draining still counts as a supply shock once voters are asked why they changed their minds.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether Brent holds above $110 through Tuesday. Whether the post-election exit polling puts energy and fuel at 25% or more of switchers' stated reasons — the pre-registered test for the fatality claim. Whether the reserve level is published before the vote.</p>
