[20:15] The contractor has a name, and the name arrived within a day

By Saturday afternoon the government had identified the company, the router and the maintenance window. What it has not done is file any of it.

What happened. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the nationwide ground stop began during maintenance by L3Harris, a long-standing federal contractor, converting a copper line to fibre at what he described as a remote location. A single router, according to the administrator of the Federal Aviation Administration, Bryan Bedford, began flooding the rest of the network, cutting communications and radar. The agency has disabled that function on the roughly sixty routers of the same type in service nationwide and is running tests with the contractor. Bedford said twice that there was “zero evidence of anything nefarious, and zero evidence of anything AI-related.” L3Harris issued a two-sentence statement saying it takes the matter seriously. The tally stands at about 8,200 cancellations, over 31 percent of Friday’s schedule, with normal operations not expected before Sunday.

Why it matters. By Saturday morning the country had two incompatible explanations for the same four hours: old equipment failing, or new work breaking an old system. Both were being used to argue for the same fifty billion dollars, and neither had been retracted. Within a day the second account won on the record and the first stopped being repeated. That is unusually fast, and it is worth noticing how it happened: not through a document, but through a secretary at a podium naming a company. No investigation has published a finding. No referral has been announced. The change record has not been released. What exists is an attribution, and an attribution from the podium is a claim, not a finding.

The pattern. A named contractor is the most convenient possible answer to a failure that needs money. It closes the question of cause in public while leaving the question of liability open in private, and it lets the argument proceed directly to the appropriation. Watch who benefits from the specificity: the agency gets a culprit and a cheque, the contractor gets a story it cannot yet rebut in public, and the Congress that has funded this system for two decades gets to be the victim of a vendor. The one party with no spokesperson in that arrangement is the flying public.

What history says. The January 2023 ground stop was traced to a contractor deleting files during a database update; it took nine days to establish and the contractor was not named from a podium. The 2001 stop was mundane. Contractor-attributed failures in infrastructure have a poor record of producing consequences: the firms are usually too embedded to be replaced, and the finding is usually that a change-control procedure was not followed. What changes outcomes is not attribution but a record. When the change record is published, the question stops being who and becomes whether the procedure was waived — and that question has an author with a name in every organisation.

What we’re watching next. Whether a hearing is noticed with a date and a committee, or merely promised. Whether the L3Harris finding reaches an official document — an inspector general referral, a contract action, a published maintenance record — rather than living in Saturday’s briefing. And whether the fifty billion becomes a number in a funding law before 11 December, or another short patch. The cost of Friday’s four hours is now being converted into a budget line, and the conversion is happening faster than the investigation.

Sources. NPR, CNN, USA Today, ABC News, The Hill.

[20:00] Israel’s spy chief flew to Washington to argue for the war America called off

The director of Mossad spent Friday in the capital, and one of the sources describing the trip says the purpose was to reverse the pause, not to bless it.

What happened. Roman Gofman, the director of Mossad and one of Benjamin Netanyahu’s closest confidants, was in Washington on Friday meeting CIA director John Ratcliffe, and was expected to travel to Miami to see the White House Middle East envoy, Steve Witkoff, according to Axios, citing three sources. Mossad, the CIA and the White House all declined to comment. The purpose, as reported, is that Netanyahu is pressing the President to resume the war against Iran. He has so far refused: the two discussed it on 5 November, and the President has instead sided with Saudi Arabia, Qatar and the Emirates on continuing the naval blockade and economic pressure while testing whether a deal is available. “Saudi Arabia has asked me to please just let it be the way it is now for a while, because they’re disintegrating,” he said on Thursday. “Qatar, Kuwait, UAE, Bahrain, all of them would much prefer the waiting period.”

Why it matters. Israel’s security cabinet ordered the military this week to step up preparations for a strike; the armed forces say preparations for renewed fighting are complete; American military aircraft are arriving in Israel; and the American military has reportedly told Israel and the Gulf states to prepare for another war — while the American president says the war is effectively over. The reason for the divergence is not strategic disagreement but domestic arithmetic. Netanyahu lost his majority in the 27 October election. Gadi Eisenkot holds the mandate to form a government, and if he succeeds Netanyahu is out. Israeli opposition figures told Axios they are worried he will attack Iran, or provoke Iran into attacking Israel, in order to break Eisenkot’s coalition. Eisenkot said publicly that he told Netanyahu he “will not allow a lack of oversight” of the prime minister’s actions during the transition, and a source close to him said he does not believe the situation requires immediate military action. On Friday the two men discussed the possibility of resuming attacks.

The pattern. This is a war being argued about by a leader who is leaving office and a leader who is trying to hold one, in opposite directions, with the same intelligence. The most useful thing about Friday is what it says about where escalation can now come from. For two weeks this record has watched for American mobilisation and found none. The mobilisation is real but it belongs to a different government, one with a collapsing mandate and a personal interest in kinetics. That is a more volatile configuration than an American decision, because it does not require a decision by the party that would absorb the consequences.

What history says. Caretaker governments launching military operations during coalition negotiations is a well-worn move, and it usually fails at the thing it is trying to do. Israel’s 1999 and 2009 transitions both featured outgoing prime ministers using the security portfolio in the gap; neither kept them in office. The distinction that matters is whether the operation is joined by the patron. An Israeli strike that the United States is informed of is one event; an Israeli strike the United States is drawn into is another, and the difference is decided by whether Washington chooses to treat it as its own.

What we’re watching next. Whether a readout names the counterpart — Ratcliffe, Witkoff, the vice president — which tells you whether this was a briefing or a negotiation. Whether Israel strikes before Eisenkot forms a government, and whether the American response is participation or a brake. And whether the President’s stated objection survives a single confirmed Iranian provocation, given that he has now described the war as over while his Treasury secretary answers Iranian threats with an obscenity. A pause that one ally is actively lobbying against has a short half-life.

Sources. Axios, The Jerusalem Post, Haaretz, Al Jazeera, Associated Press.

[19:45] Three leaders, one room, no inspectors

The first three-way summit of the nuclear age has a date, a venue and both counterparts confirmed. It has no verification language anywhere in it.

What happened. The White House and the Kremlin both confirmed that the President, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin will meet Thursday on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen on nuclear non-proliferation. It would be the first time the heads of the three largest nuclear arsenals have sat in the same room. The President has said he believes the meeting could produce a new agreement among them. No framework, no agenda item, no preconditions and no drafting process have been described by any of the three governments.

Why it matters. Yesterday this was an announcement of a meeting without a venue, and neither counterpart had confirmed attendance. Both have now confirmed and the venue is a place. That is real movement and should be recorded as such. What has not moved is the thing that distinguishes an arms agreement from a photograph: nothing in any account of the meeting mentions inspections, telemetry, data exchange or a monitoring body, and the one existing arrangement that provides any of it — the New START data-sharing mechanism — has lapsed. Three leaders can agree to reduce warheads in an afternoon. Verifying it takes years and an institution.

The pattern. Summits of this shape are usually convened for what they signal rather than what they sign. Announcing that the three biggest arsenals will meet sets a price on the meeting itself, and the price is paid by whoever needs it not to fail — a president who has just lost both chambers, a Russian economy dependent on an American waiver, a Chinese leadership managing an export-control fight over semiconductors. The absence of verification language is not an oversight in a document this anticipated; it is a decision about what the meeting is for. A meeting that produces numbers can be claimed. A meeting that produces inspections can be failed.

What history says. The 1986 Reykjavík summit came closer to eliminating arsenals than any meeting before or since and failed on one word: verification. SALT I took three years of talks to produce a treaty whose monitoring was built on satellites both sides already had. The pattern is consistent — the declaration is cheap, the architecture is expensive, and every genuine reduction has been preceded by an inspection regime rather than announced with one. A first-ever trilateral is a large event. It is not, on any precedent in this record, an instrument.

What we’re watching next. Whether any document out of Shenzhen names verification, inspection or telemetry rather than “cuts.” Whether the three produce a joint statement at all, and whether it is issued by governments or by spokesmen. And whether the meeting is used to ratify something already agreed by deputies, which is what a successful summit usually is, or to substitute for it, which is what a summit usually becomes when the deputies failed.

Sources. Associated Press, NBC News, BBC, CNN, Al Jazeera.

[19:30] The measles status was lost, and the response had already been declared unchanged

Twenty-six years of elimination ended this week in a decision taken by a regional health body. The domestic agency had already said the outcome would not change what it does.

What happened. The Pan American Health Organization has revoked the United States’ measles-elimination status, ending a run that began in 2000, after concluding the country failed to stop continuous transmission for more than twelve months. The CDC has recorded 5,412 cases this year and six deaths — the agency changed how it counts death reports from states, and state reporting puts the figure at ten. There have been more than 590 hospitalisations. Pennsylvania has led the case count. The director of the CDC said in November that the agency would not fundamentally change its outbreak response even if the status were lost.

Why it matters. Elimination is not the absence of measles; it is a certification that any case can be traced to an imported one and contained. Losing it means the virus is now circulating domestically, which changes what every subsequent outbreak costs to handle and puts the country in a category with a small number of others. The confirmation is not the decision but the sentence that preceded it. The status was lost, and the response was announced in advance to be unchanged. That is the whole story: a measurable institutional threshold was crossed and the institution had already declared the threshold non-operative. Six deaths or ten, on a counting method the agency revised, is the second-order finding — when the numbers get inconvenient, the method changes and nobody is required to say so.

The pattern. This is what the subtraction series is for. Nothing was banned, no agency was abolished, no order was signed. A designation lapsed because a technical condition was met, and the machinery that would have responded to it had been quietly reconfigured to be indifferent. Enclosure by abandonment rather than by seizure: the state does not take the capability away, it declines to use it, and the absence is then unfalsifiable because nothing happens on the day. The most consequential events in this record are the ones with no press conference, and this is the fourth in a row.

What history says. The United States has never lost this status before, though it came close in 2019 with nearly 1,300 cases and held on. The regional precedents are not encouraging in either direction. Brazil lost its status in 2019 and regained it in 2024. Venezuela lost it in 2018 and was reverified in 2023 after five years of sustained effort. Canada lost its designation in 2025 and, in this year, declared its multi-jurisdictional outbreak over in October — so restoration is possible, and every case of it involved an intensified vaccination campaign and a rebuilt surveillance system, run by a government that treated the loss as an emergency. That is the variable here: not whether the status can be regained, but whether anyone has said they intend to.

What we’re watching next. Whether any state or the CDC announces a vaccination push, which would indicate the designation still functions as an incentive. Whether the death count discrepancy between the agency and the states is reconciled, and by whom. And whether the Pope’s tour through South America, where vaccination rates are low and crowds are large, produces an outbreak that forces the question into a different room. A public health system’s capacity is best measured by what it does after it is told it has failed.

Sources. CNN, NPR, CBS News, KFF Health News, Axios.

[11:20] Three thousand flights are still cancelled, and the explanation has split in two

The planes stopped for four hours on Friday. By Saturday morning there were two different causes in circulation, and both were being used to argue for the same money.

What happened. More than three thousand US flights are cancelled this morning as airlines work through the backlog from Friday’s nationwide ground stop, which ran about four hours and was lifted at 8:28pm Eastern. College football moved with it: the Southeastern Conference pushed the Mississippi–Oklahoma game to Sunday, and Oklahoma’s athletic director attacked the decision in public. Overnight, the most widely carried story in the country was a Reuters examination reporting that old air traffic control equipment regularly disrupts flights — a chronic condition rather than a one-off accident.

Why it matters. That does not sit easily with what two cabinet secretaries said from the podium on Friday night: that new equipment had overwhelmed an older system, that switches and routers returned inconsistent data, and that the agency shut the system down because of it. Old equipment breaking down and new equipment breaking the system are not the same claim. Neither has been withdrawn. And a Republican congresswoman, Marjorie Taylor Greene, called Friday’s briefing “a shakedown for more money,” while the top Democrat on the House Budget Committee said he would back a major appropriation to overhaul the system. The fight over the money started before anyone established the cause.

The pattern. A failure that everyone experiences becomes an argument that everyone can join. The cause is still open, so the vacancy gets filled by whichever explanation is useful. That is not a conspiracy; it is what happens when an institution asks for fifty billion dollars in the same press conference in which it admits it does not yet know what went wrong. The two accounts can both be partly true — old equipment can be fragile and a new update can still break it — but only one of them is being checked.

What history says. The two previous national ground stops, in 2001 and January 2023, both ended in mundane causes and neither produced money quickly. The 2023 stop, about ninety minutes long, was caused by a contractor deleting files during a database update; the root cause took nine days to establish. The difference this time is not secrecy but daylight: Friday’s silence happened across drive time into the evening, when millions of people were awake to experience it. A failure people feel is a failure people fund.

What we’re watching next. Whether a congressional committee notices a hearing by Monday, which tells you whether the agency wants an inquiry or wants the cheque. Whether the name of the company running the FAA’s private network appears in an official document. And whether the modernisation money arrives as a long-term funding law before 11 December, or as another four-week patch that settles nothing. An emergency of this size that produces no rule, no order and no hearing within a day is itself a reading.

Update, 8:15pm. The split closed within the day, and it closed in the direction of a named vendor: the Transportation Secretary attributed Friday’s failure to maintenance by L3Harris at a remote site, and the FAA administrator said a single router flooded the network — see the 20:15 card. The political argument did not close with it. The request went from “billions” to fifty billion dollars, and a promised hearing has no date, no committee and no notice.

Sources. Associated Press, Reuters, The Hill, CNN.

[11:05] The strike on Iran is off, for now. The water is still being described as mined.

A pause announced by sources, three mine claims announced by Tehran, and a market that has already decided which one to believe.

What happened. Reuters reports, from sources, that the President has told advisers he may hold off on attacking Iran unless there is a new provocation. The same morning’s reporting has him describing the war as “effectively over” while noting it is still costing the United States billions of dollars a month, and his Treasury Secretary answering a question about Iranian threats with an obscenity. Against all of that, Iran’s navy says an oil tanker is on fire in the Strait of Hormuz after hitting a mine, and Iranian outlets claim attacks on an American radar station in Bahrain and on a gas tanker in the strait. No government with a warship in the water has confirmed any of it.

Why it matters. A war that is declared over while mines are still being reported in the shipping lane it was fought over is not over; it is unannounced. The reason to take the pause seriously is that it comes with a cost attached — the shooting has stopped, the ships are still there, and the bill keeps arriving. The reason to doubt it is that the same week produced a threat to double tariffs on Mexico and a plan for a three-leader summit, which is what a government does when it wants the war off the front page rather than off the map.

The pattern. Wars rarely end with a document. They thin out. Declarations, then pauses, then a negotiation that neither side will call a negotiation, while the physical violence continues at a lower level and gets attributed by whoever benefits. The tell is not the announcement; it is who confirms the violence. Every claim in the water this morning is being made by Iran about Iran’s own coast, which means the record of what is happening there belongs to one party.

What history says. The 2023–24 Red Sea campaign followed this shape for months: announced operations, unattributed attacks, insurance rates doing the actual reporting. The 1991 Gulf ceasefire was followed by a decade of enforcement that nobody called war. A pause that is declared by one side’s sources, unverified by the other, and contradicted by explosions in the same waterway should be read as a change in intensity, not an end state.

What we’re watching next. Whether any strike is attributed on the record by both sides, which would break the pause in a way that cannot be walked back. Whether a flag state or an insurer confirms a mine, which would force the shipping trade to reprice. And whether the planned three-leader summit produces a venue and a format, or stays a schedule reported as a settlement.

Sources. Reuters, The Hill, Al Jazeera, Associated Press.

[10:50] Alaska answered three questions at once, and none of the answers helped the claim

A Democrat won the governorship, a Senate seat is slipping away, and voters kept the voting system they were asked to abolish.

What happened. Three results landed overnight. Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins won Alaska’s governorship — the first Democrat elected to that office since 1998, replacing a Republican. Mary Peltola holds a lead of nearly four percentage points over Senator Dan Sullivan in the latest batch of counted ballots. And Ballot Measure 638 failed, so Alaska keeps its open primaries and its ranked-choice voting system.

Why it matters. For a week the losing side in Alaska has said, in public and by name, that the Senate race was stolen — that the result was obvious and that ballots were being counted to produce a different answer. Meanwhile the count has kept moving, in public, the other way. The measure that would have changed how Alaska counts was on the same ballot and was rejected by Alaska’s own voters. Nobody has refused to accept a certified result. That is the news, and it is a null: the machinery was tested where the allegation was loudest and it held.

The pattern. Election challenges need two things to become dangerous: a close count, and an institution willing to carry the claim. Alaska has had the first for a week and never found the second. In Mississippi the incumbent who first refused the count conceded before certification. In Alaska the claimant’s own numbers moved away from him instead of toward him. The claim did not fail because it was disproved; it failed because the arithmetic ran out while it was being made.

What history says. Contested counts become crises when a party’s institutional actors adopt them — Florida in 2000 had a legal army on both sides, and the outcome was decided by a court rather than by a mob. Where the challenge stays with a candidate, it usually dies at certification. The variable that matters is not the margin but whether anyone with authority picks the claim up.

What we’re watching next. Whether Peltola’s lead holds as the remaining ballots drop, and whether the final margin falls inside the number of rejected ballots that a court declined to order disclosed — that is the one place a late reversal could still come from. And whether any named national Republican refuses a certified result somewhere else before 31 December. So far the count is zero.

Update, 8:15pm. Correction to the word “null” above: with neither candidate above fifty percent, Alaska runs its ranked-choice tabulation on 18 November, and estimates of the second-choice redistribution disagree — one partial count has Senator Sullivan netting about nine hundred votes, another has the third candidate’s voters preferring Peltola. The claim has found no institutional carrier so far. That is not the same as having been refused, and the test cannot be scored until the tabulation runs.

Sources. Associated Press, NPR, The Hill.

[10:35] The witness agreed to come, but the second file opened first

The former Nvidia chief will testify. Overnight, a second workplace relationship was reported, and the question the hearing was meant to answer is being answered elsewhere.

What happened. Jensen Huang, removed as Nvidia’s chief executive nine days ago, has agreed to testify before Congress; the Senate Banking Committee has set 3 December. Overnight, Reuters reported from sources that he had a relationship with a second Nvidia employee, in addition to the Chinese businesswoman at the centre of the affair that began all of this. Elon Musk said “critical deals will be lost” without him, and the President is taking Musk on his Asia trip while planning not to discuss the matter.

Why it matters. The hearing was supposed to settle a policy question: whether Nvidia’s most advanced computing was being rented to Chinese customers abroad rather than shipped to them, which would route around export controls without breaking them. That question is now being answered in the press, with the Treasury Secretary blaming the Commerce Department for the missing controls — a dispute between two agencies, which produces a memorandum rather than a rule. The witness who could answer it under oath now arrives with a personal file that will consume the questioning.

The pattern. This is how a policy failure gets converted into a personnel story. The board moved first, in nine days, before any regulator acted; the state’s compulsory process arrives three weeks later, in public, after the only decision that mattered has been made. A removal settles the market’s question and buries the government’s. The hearing will produce a transcript, and the loophole will still be open.

What history says. Congressional hearings on financial and technological failures rarely produce rules; they produce narratives, and the narrative usually lands on the person rather than the structure. The 2008 hearings on bank executives are the template: memorable, quotable, and followed by legislation that had already been drafted elsewhere by other people. The instrument that changes behaviour is the rule, and the rule requires an agency willing to write it while the story is hot.

What we’re watching next. Whether the agreement names the Banking Committee and the 3 December date specifically, or stays a general offer. Whether the questioning reaches rented capacity or stays on the affair. And whether Commerce produces an export-control rule before the hearing, which would make the hearing a formality — or after it, which would make it theatre.

Sources. Reuters, The Hill, CNN, Breitbart.

[10:20] America lost its measles-free status, and nothing happened on the day it did

A designation the country held for twenty-six years expired quietly. It is the longest-lasting thing in this morning’s news and the least covered.

What happened. The United States has lost its measles-elimination status, according to reporting carried this morning by Reuters and Deutsche Welle from the Wall Street Journal. Elimination status is a formal public-health designation: it means the country went twelve months without continuous transmission of a disease it had previously stamped out. The country had held it since 2000.

Why it matters. Losing it is not a warning about the future; it is a finding about the recent past. It means measles has been circulating continuously inside the United States for a year, in pockets, without being stopped. It also means the machinery that stopped it before — surveillance, rapid response, and a vaccination rate high enough that outbreaks cannot find fuel — no longer works reliably enough to interrupt transmission. That is a capacity failure, and capacity failures do not announce themselves.

The pattern. This is the fourth item in a series that never gets a headline: HIV prevention funding cut for a state, billions in global health money redirected against what Congress intended, public-health staff cut in state after state, and now a designation lost. Each one is a subtraction. Nothing happens on the day the money goes; the consequence arrives years later, in a population that has never seen the disease and a vaccination rate that takes a decade to rebuild. Subtraction is invisible until it is an outbreak.

What history says. Measles was declared eliminated in the United States in 2000 after decades of vaccination. It has been re-established in countries that let coverage fall — the United Kingdom lost its status in 2018 and took years to recover it. The disease is among the most contagious known; it finds the unvaccinated with extraordinary efficiency. Elimination is not a trophy, it is a maintained condition, and maintenance looks exactly like spending money on nothing happening.

What we’re watching next. Whether any state or the Centers for Disease Control responds with a vaccination requirement, a funding restoration or a public campaign — or whether the status simply lapses into a fact nobody acts on. And whether the next outbreak is attributed to the lapse, which would be the moment the subtraction becomes visible.

Sources. Reuters, Deutsche Welle, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, CNN.
