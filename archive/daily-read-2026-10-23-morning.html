Trump says he personally ordered the Justice Department to sue over the Alaska Senate race

The Justice Department filed a federal lawsuit asking a judge to stop Alaska from certifying its Senate election if Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan loses to Democrat Mary Peltola but “would have won” counting votes cast for a second candidate also named Daniel J. Sullivan. The suit argues “voter confusion” could tip one of the closest races in the country. Asked about it Friday, Trump said plainly: “I’m the one that said do it.”

Why it matters: This is the first time a president has personally claimed credit for a pre-election lawsuit to block certification of a Senate race. It removes the official deniability that the Justice Department acts independently — the department’s leadership had insisted it was not directed by the White House. The suit was filed 11 days before Election Day, before any votes could be miscounted.

The pattern: The federal government is normally the party that certifies results stand unless a loser proves fraud after the fact. Here, the Justice Department asks a court to pre-bank an order that would stop certification before the result is even known — reversing the burden of proof ahead of time.

History says: Election litigation in past cycles came after votes were counted, by losing campaigns. Governments have pre-cleared legal instruments for contested elections in weaker democracies — the legal action is prepared in advance so it can be used the moment it is needed.

Watching next: Whether the Alaska court acts before November 3, whether similar suits appear in other close states, and whether the second Sullivan candidate — who denies coordinating — is ever traced to a sponsor.

Sources: Politico, New York Times, BBC, Guardian

Justice Department asks court to block counting of 20,000 mail ballots in California

In a parallel case, the Justice Department sued California claiming more than 20,000 registered voters are “likely” noncitizens, and asked a federal court to bar the state from counting mail ballots cast by anyone on a Justice Department-supplied list. It has also warned county election officials they could face prosecution for processing those ballots. Attorney General Todd Blanche said the department could seek to block election certification outright in a “doomsday scenario.” Los Angeles County officials say the demand would make it “virtually impossible” to certify results on time.

Why it matters: Two suit types now aim at the same target: counting and certifying. In California the mechanism is slowing or halting the count of contested ballots; in Alaska it is pre-blocking certification of the result. Both raise the stakes for officials who administer elections on the ground.

The pattern: The claim of large-scale noncitizen voting has been repeatedly investigated in past elections and found to be vanishingly rare. A list supplied in advance, that officials must exclude on federal demand, creates a mechanism where the federal government chooses whose ballots count.

History says: Accuracy concerns are best handled before ballots are mailed, not by singling out tens of thousands of ballots for exclusion after they are cast. Historically, vague lists of “suspect voters” have skewed heavily against naturalized citizens and minority communities.

Watching next: Whether courts act before November 3, whether California slows or changes its counting timeline, and whether the “doomsday scenario” language reappears as a standing justification.

Sources: AP via PBS, USA Today, The Hill, PBS

Top Republicans privately concede the House, with 11 days to go

Politico reports that leading House Republicans are privately telling donors and allies they expect to lose the chamber in November, citing the economy, broken promises on prices, and a base that appears less energized than in 2024. Publicly, party leaders continue to predict victory.

Why it matters: A House concession two weeks before the election is remarkable on its own — but it interacts with the election lawsuits above. If leadership expects to lose, legal tools to slow or block certification become the contingency plan rather than a way to win.

The pattern: Parties that expect a defeat have an incentive to pre-frame it. If results are contested on Election Night, the “color revolution” narrative that has been built in right-wing media supplies a ready-made explanation for a loss that polling already anticipated.

History says: In 2020, pre-positioned claims that the election was stolen shaped public belief long after courts rejected them. Pre-framing a loss as theft is a strategy with a proven track record of persuading a partisan base regardless of the actual count.

Watching next: Whether the private concession becomes public messaging, and how candidate rhetoric changes as Election Day nears.

Sources: Politico

“Color revolution” frame floods right-wing media days before Election Day

A widely circulated “evidence drop” — built on unnamed sources and unpublished documents — claims the country faces a foreign-style “color revolution” and that proof will be revealed after the election. The framework was seeded by commentators over the past year and is now being harvested: shared by millions of accounts in the final days before voting.

Why it matters: A narrative that pre-justifies treating a defeat as a coup is most effective when it arrives just before the vote, so it is fresh rather than stale. It requires no evidence to spread — only a promise of evidence later.

The pattern: Seeding followed by harvest: the interpretive frame is built quietly over months, then activated with a coordinated release at the moment of maximum effect. Its signature feature is self-sealing — anyone who disputes it is said to be part of the plot.

History says: Movements that pre-plan to reject an election result always build the story before the result exists. The tell is not the claim itself but the timing: saturation days before voting, with verification always promised for after.

Watching next: How the frame converges with the administration’s lawsuits — whether courts that block certification will be cast as resisting the “revolution” rather than upholding the count.

Sources: Media Matters, Washington Post
