## Lead: Putin says there will be no negotiations, and answers the bridge strike on three Kyiv bridges and in Polish airspace

**The facts.** Putin, speaking after the Shenzhen trilateral, said "there will be no negotiations" and promised a response "far more painful" than Ukraine's strike on the Crimean Bridge (CNN, BBC). Overnight Russia fired dozens of ballistic missiles and hundreds of drones: three bridges across the Dnipro in Kyiv were hit, at least nine dead (BBC, Bloomberg, NBC). Then two Russian cruise missiles and a jet-powered drone came down inside Poland — a Kh-101 in Nowy Lipowiec, Lublin province, and a second object near Rudnik nad Sanem (Guardian, BBC, AFP, Notes from Poland, AP). Tusk called it an "intentional provocation" timed to the Coalition of the Willing exercises being held in Poland for a possible postwar mission in Ukraine. No casualties reported. Three Russian objects, three NATO countries' worth of decision.

**Why it leads.** Two days ago the settlement leg was being read upward on a "peace declaration" from the trilateral. Today the party to that declaration says there will be no negotiations and demonstrates what he means by hitting the capital's river crossings — the same target class Ukraine used against his bridge. A settlement leg that cannot survive forty-eight hours past its own communiqué is not a settlement leg. That is the finding, and it is worth more than any of the individual strikes.

**The Poland object.** A missile that lands in NATO territory during an exercise is a test with a built-in opt-out: it can always be called an accident, and Poland's own framing — "provocation", not "attack" — is the tell that Warsaw has taken the opt-out. Article 4 consultations are cheap; Article 5 is not being discussed. The July incursion was the same missile type and produced the same sentence. A signature that repeats is a signature, not an accident, and the absence of a response is what makes repetition cheap.

**What it does to the card.** `eurasia-settlement-hemisphere-pivot-domestic-consolidation` moves 74 to 70. Not a falsifier hit — the falsifier is a reserve tasking order, and nothing today touches it. But the settlement leg's forward motion has stopped and reversed inside the same week it started, and the card charges for the sequence, not for the words.

**What I am not doing.** I am not scoring the Poland incursion as an escalation. There is no named response, no consultation under Article 4 announced, no force posture change. An object in a field is a fact; an alliance decision is an event. Today produced the first, not the second.

The political clock runs to the seating of the 120th Congress; the military clock runs to how long Iran's rebuild stays targetable. Whoever is briefing CBS is arguing that the second clock is winning.

**The chemical-weapons line.** The Iraqi-WMD analogy is being made by Tehran, not by us, and it is a good analogy: a WMD claim offered without evidence, ahead of a decision. ## 4. The Senate passes permitting reform 62–33, and names the reason

**The facts.** The Senate passed S.5653, the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, 62–33, on Thursday night: faster permitting for power plants, grid upgrades, pipelines, mines and data centres (Politico, NYT, NPR, Axios). NPR's framing is explicit — rising energy costs and demand driven by AI. It goes to the House.

**Why it matters.** This is the substrate leg producing an instrument. A permitting statute is the slowest-moving thing a state does and the most durable: it changes what can be built for a decade, and it removes procedural friction rather than adding money. It is also, in a week when the war powers resolution failed and the funding fight hardened, the one bipartisan thing that passed — which is the opposite of the story the churn gauge tells.

**Where it sits against the cards.** No card moves. `agi-cognitive-labor-repricing` (65) and the AI-substrate cards are about labour and capital, not electricity. But an energy statute written because of data-centre demand is a substrate commitment that no AI firm had to make, and it belongs on the watch list as the first policy act of the cycle that treats compute load as infrastructure.

## 7. The World Court finds a state committed genocide, and the state calls the ruling unfair

**The facts.** The ICJ found Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya (Reuters, AP, Al Jazeera, DW). Myanmar called the ruling "unfair" (Reuters, 4 outlets).

**Why it matters.** A finding of state responsibility for genocide is one of the rarest outputs the international system produces, and it changes the legal status of every subsequent act rather than the facts on the ground. The response — "unfair" — is the standard one, and it is the reason these rulings do little: the court has no enforcement arm.
