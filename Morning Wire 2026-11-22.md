## Lead: The contractors are told to check with the White House before answering Congress

**The facts.** Trump has ordered federal contractors to consult the White House before responding to congressional inquiries (AP, 6 outlets: AP, Breitbart, CNN, Just The News, NPR, The Hill). It is the second-broadest story in the sweep.

**Why it leads.** A contractor answering a congressional committee is a private party performing a public function, speaking to the body that appropriates the money. Routing that answer through the executive first does three things at once: it gives the White House pre-publication review of what Congress learns, it makes the contractor's contract the price of candour, and it converts an oversight channel into a permission channel. No subpoena is defied. Nothing is refused in writing. The information simply arrives later, filtered, and attributed to a party who can be punished for what it says.

**Why it is the same instrument as the arrest rumour.** Sam flagged a low-level circulating plan to begin arresting political opposition. This is not that. It is the cheaper adjacent move: you do not need to arrest an opponent if you can make every organisation that would finance, staff or speak for one ask permission first. Gag on the contracting layer is the soft version of the same objective, and soft versions are the ones that survive court challenge.

**What would make it hard.** Contractors are not employees. A directive to a private firm to clear speech with the government runs into the same wall as the 2025 funding-condition fights: the government may choose whom it buys from, but conditioning speech is a different power than conditioning payment, and the line between them is exactly what litigation tests. Watch for the directive's *form*. An OMB memo is policy; a contract clause is enforceable; a phone call is deniable and shapes behaviour fastest.

**What I am not doing.** Not scoring it. No dated catalyst, no principal named, so it belongs in observation and the enforcement series, not in the prediction register.

## 2. The custody net now includes people who were never the target

**The facts.** Washington's governor and a union denounced ICE arrests of travellers at Seattle-Tacoma airport (Reuters, 8 outlets — the broadest story of the morning). Regional-only items with no national carrier: a Princeton student detained at Reagan Airport (CBS); a Chinese-born neuroscientist detained while travelling; a federal judge in New York denying release to a man shot by immigration agents; a New York court filing in which the government admits an Austin man it deported held dual citizenship; the last migrant shelter in New York City closing; protesters facing ICE agents in Van Nuys the day after a detention in Santa Clarita.

**Why it matters.** An airport is the one place where status is checked by definition, which makes it the cheapest place to widen a net without new legal authority. Students, scientists and travellers are not the enforcement target named in the programme — they are the proof that the check itself has become the policy. That distinction matters for the courts: a programme aimed at removable persons with criminal histories is defensible; a checkpoint at a departure gate is a different legal animal and produces different plaintiffs.

**The union is the signal.** A governor objecting is politics. A union objecting means its members are being asked to do something they think is unlawful or unsafe, and unions put that in writing to protect themselves. Watch for the union's demand to be specific: an instruction to refuse, a request for written legal cover, or a grievance. Any of the three is a document.

**Card effect.** None, but it is the fourth consecutive week in which the enforcement record grew by city rather than by statute. That is the pattern worth the series.

## 3. The FBI's criminal justice database, and the president's spin

**The facts.** The FBI is investigating an apparent hack of its key national criminal justice database, per an AP source. CNN reports Trump attempting to put a "positive spin" on the apparent breach. Three outlets carry it.

**Why this is the item and not a footnote.** This database is the connective tissue of domestic policing: it is what makes a record in one state visible to an officer in another. If it was accessed rather than merely probed, the exposure is not data loss, it is the integrity of the queries — a corrupted or injected record propagates silently into arrests, bail decisions and background checks for years. There is no user-facing outage to announce, which is why the political response is spin rather than a status page.

**The tell.** A president choosing to characterise a breach before the bureau has characterised it is a decision about the story, not the system. Compare the week's other failures: Microsoft published an incident number and named the failing component. Here, an anonymous source and a framing.

**What stays open.** Whether records were read, altered, or only enumerated. Those are three different incidents with three different remediations and the public account does not yet distinguish them.

## 5. Voters removed the sheriffs who signed the pacts

**The facts.** Voters ousted several sheriffs who had signed agreements to assist federal immigration enforcement (AP, 3 outlets).

**Why it matters.** This is the first electoral return on the enforcement programme, and it runs opposite to the national result. Sheriffs are the most locally accountable law-enforcement office in the country — elected, small constituencies, no national media. If cooperation agreements cost sheriffs their seats, the voluntary layer of the programme thins out precisely where it must be executed, and the federal response to that is usually to substitute federal officers for local ones. That substitution is what produces the airport and courthouse arrests in item 2.

**What to watch.** Whether the incoming sheriffs formally withdraw from the agreements, and whether any state legislature moves to prohibit them. Withdrawal is a document; a platform is not.

## 6. Virginia reports the first child measles death of 2026

**The facts.** Virginia reports a young child as the state's first measles death in 2026 (AP, 3 outlets). This follows the loss of US measles elimination status, certified on 18 November.

**Why it matters.** Elimination status is a certification about transmission chains; a paediatric death is the human ledger entry behind it. They are not the same fact and the second one changes the politics: a certification is contestable by officials, a dead child is not. Expect the state-health and school-vaccination fights to sharpen within the week.
