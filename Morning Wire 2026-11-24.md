## Lead: Iran claims mines under escort, and orders the strait shut "day by day"

**Facts.** The IRGC said Monday that three oil tankers in the Strait of Hormuz suffered "powerful explosions and massive fires" after hitting naval mines — one off the southern shore, two in the east of the strait, "while under extensive air escort by the US army." The Joint Maritime Information Centre's report for 19-21 November recorded 114 passages "with the assistance of the United States" and said the mine claims were unconfirmed. Iran's army commander-in-chief, Maj. Gen. Amir Hatami, ordered the navy to close the strait "further day by day" until seven Iranian conditions are met, and warned that a new US action would bring blows "more severe, more crushing and without geographical limitation"; he attributed the order to Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei. Parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf: no negotiation before the conditions are implemented, and there are "military ways to break the blockade" to be pursued "in the near future." Foreign ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei called the blockade "tantamount to military aggression" (Euronews Persian, Reuters, AP).

**Why it leads.** The two-track structure is now explicit in the same 24 hours: Qatar's prime minister met Rubio and then Vance (ten minutes, per AP's source) to restart talks, while Iran's military command ordered a wider closure and claimed hits on ships being escorted. Trump said Sunday that strikes could resume after the New Year. The claim is doing work whether or not it is true: an unconfirmed mine attack on escorted shipping is precisely the item that insurers and flag states cannot ignore, and JMIC's own unconfirmed label is the only thing standing between the claim and a war-risk repricing.

**Pattern.** Widening by notice. The blockade has been extended three times since the war began, each time by administrative definition rather than by an announced operation — most recently to any vessel carrying Iranian cargo by ship-to-ship transfer. Iran's answer has the same shape: a standing order to tighten "day by day" rather than a declared campaign. Neither side has to name a phase to keep escalating.

**Card effect.** `iran-escalation-node1-fires` holds at 56. Hatami's phrase "in the new phase" is conditional — it describes what follows a new US action, not a phase that has opened. ## 2. Correction: the Army asked the court, and the court said yes

**Facts.** Trump on Monday ordered the military to hold off on the Iowa detonation pending the Eighth Circuit, deferring to the court and to Governor-elect Rob Sand. The appeals court then denied the environmental groups' emergency request and cleared the Army's permit. On Monday evening acting Army Secretary Adam Telle said the Army would proceed with a "controlled removal" of 68 barrels of lead azide — about 9,500 pounds — at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown on Wednesday, calling it "a consensus decision" of the agencies involved. The plan's own assessment has a dust plume carrying lead particles extending up to 100 miles. The Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club's Iowa chapter had argued EPA skipped required permitting steps (CNN, AP, PBS, Guardian, NBC, WaPo, CBS).

**Why it matters.** This is the clean test yesterday's wire said it would be, and it resolved toward compliance. The executive asserted an emergency exception in a filing on 22 November, then paused when the president said to, sought the court's leave, and proceeded only after the court granted it. The emergency power was asserted and not used. That is data against the "court order as comment period" reading and it should be recorded as such before the next test makes the opposite point.

**Why the substance still matters.** Lead azide is shock-sensitive and has been degrading in bunkers whose blast doors were removed; the Army's own case is that an uncontrolled detonation is the larger risk. Both the county and the state fought the on-site burn for months. A 100-mile dust plume over farmland is a real harm and the court accepted the agency's alternative analysis. Nothing about the compliance finding makes the blast safe.

**What history says.** The non-compliance tell is act first, litigate after — Jackson and Worcester, 1832; the 1974 subpoena fight is the counterexample where the order was the operative instrument and the presidency broke on it. Here the order was the instrument. Note the asymmetry for the gate record: the executive can comply at low cost on a fact pattern where it loses nothing politically, which is why a single compliant instance cannot carry the D1 channel either.

## 4. The Omar files: a judge's warnings and a lawyer who would not take part

**Facts.** A federal judge unsealed more than 160 pages of heavily redacted records late Monday from the 12 October Homeland Security Investigations search of Rep. Ilhan Omar's Washington residence. The judge who signed the warrant had repeatedly expressed reservations that DOJ had not adequately considered the protections owed to a member of Congress. DOJ's filings say the House's top lawyer "refused to be involved" before the search was executed; at least one judge described a DOJ argument as one that "blinks reality." Omar's attorneys say the government failed to give required notice and initially kept her from counsel (Politico, NBC, WaPo, MPR).

**Why it matters.** The protective procedure — notice to the House, accommodation of the Speech or Debate privilege — was not a formality the department forgot; on this record it was raised, pressed by the judge, and then routed around. Whatever the merits of the underlying immigration inquiry, the constitutional question is now documentary rather than alleged, and it is the House's own lawyer whose non-participation is being used as the explanation.

**What history says.** The 2006 FBI search of Rep. William Jefferson's office was the first raid on a member's office in history; both parties objected, the materials were sealed for 18 days, and the fight ended in a court-ordered review process rather than a clean win for either branch. The precedent established that the separation-of-powers problem is procedural — who reviews, before what is read — and that is exactly the stage this record is at.

## 5. The airport has become the checkpoint

**Facts.** Masked ICE agents detained a Mexican father and his daughter on the jet bridge at Seattle-Tacoma on Saturday as they boarded an Alaska Airlines flight to Los Angeles. Governor Bob Ferguson called ICE "completely and totally out of control," said he is looking into how agents reached the jet bridge, and suggested the operation violated the state's 2026 law limiting facial coverings by law enforcement; the Port of Seattle called the scene distressing and said it would engage federal officials (AP, CNN, CBS, Guardian, Al Jazeera). Separately, Eva Yi Xie, a third-year Princeton neuroscience doctoral student and Chinese national, was detained at Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport on Thursday while flying back from a conference and remains in ICE custody (NYT, AP, NBC). Documents obtained by American Oversight show a formal TSA-ICE data-sharing agreement in place since May 2025, under which, per Reuters, TSA supplied records on more than 31,000 travellers and ICE made more than 800 arrests from TSA tips through February 2026.

**Why it matters.** These are three stages of one pipeline: the data before the flight, the arrest at the gate, and the custody after it. The airport was the one place where federal authority stopped at a boundary; it is now an interior checkpoint with a captive audience, and the legal question — how officers reached a jet bridge, whether masks complied with state law, whether a student visa status justifies detention at a domestic gate — is being litigated after the fact, one passenger at a time.

**What history says.** NSEERS, the post-9/11 special registration programme, turned a registration requirement into an enforcement dragnet: 83,000 men registered, about 13,000 faced removal proceedings, and the programme was abandoned in 2016 because it produced enforcement rather than security. The lesson recorded then is the one at issue now: once a routine administrative touchpoint is wired to removal, the volume of enforcement tracks the size of the database, not the threat.

**What history says.** Marbury v. Madison established the power to review and then declined to exercise it, because the route to the courthouse was wrong. The case is remembered for the power; the outcome was a loss. Forum is not a detail of the remedy, it is the remedy.

## 7. Ukrainian sea drones reach a Russian warship in Libya

**Facts.** Ukrainian naval drones struck the Russian corvette Merkury at its berth in Benghazi on Sunday. Zelensky said seven drones were used and the ship was sunk; two Libyan officials said it was severely damaged; the SBU released approach footage showing the impact and explosions. The eastern Libyan government called the attack a violation of maritime safety and of its sovereignty. Russia has used eastern Libya as a Mediterranean logistics and naval support point (AP, BBC, NPR, Guardian, NBC, NYT).

**Why it matters.** This is a Russian naval asset destroyed in a third country's port, more than 2,000 km from Ukraine, without the host government's consent — and the host government is Russia's client. Every Russian ship in the Mediterranean is now a target it cannot escort everywhere, and every government hosting one has acquired a new liability.

**What history says.** Tsushima, 1905: a fleet that had sailed halfway around the world was destroyed in an afternoon because it had to be somewhere. The 1980s Tanker War added the modern corollary — when the sea is contested, the cost lands on insurers and flag states before it lands on navies.
