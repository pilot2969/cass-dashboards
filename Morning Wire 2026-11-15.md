## Lead: the FAA was warned for years, and the hearing moved to December

Two facts landed overnight and they point the same way.

**Warning, not surprise.** A Washington Post investigation published this morning (Frankel, Duncan, Scott) reports the FAA had been warned for years about the repair risks that produced Friday's ground stop. That is the first document, as opposed to an attribution, in the whole affair. It changes what Thursday morning's fight is about. Duffy's podium answer — a contractor's maintenance mistake at a remote location — was a novelty claim: an unforeseeable error. A multi-year warning record makes it a known-risk claim, and known risk has an owner at every level: the agency that was warned, the department that funded the patch, and the Congress that has run the system on continuing resolutions for two decades. This is the difference between "a router failed" and "we were told the routers would fail."

## 1. Peoria: the device was real, the FBI has it, and nobody has been named

The story we missed entirely yesterday is now the most serious domestic security item of the week, and the detail that matters arrived overnight.

The FBI confirmed late Saturday that an **explosive device** was indeed found at BioUrja Renewables, and that it is now **leading** the investigation, with ATF, Illinois State Police and the Peoria County Sheriff. The evacuation ran **18 hours** and covered a one-mile radius — parts of downtown Peoria, the Warehouse District and East Peoria — displacing roughly 5,000 people. That is the largest evacuation in central Illinois in years. No other devices were found. The plant received the threat electronically hours before the device was located; bomb techs rendered it safe at 9:12pm Friday. The FBI had scheduled a briefing for 10:30 this morning in Springfield and **postponed it just before 9am**, with no new date.

Why that postponement is the detail to hold: an investigation that cancels its own press conference on the morning of is an investigation that has something it is not ready to say. It is also consistent with nothing being established. Both are true, and the difference will be visible in whether a name appears this week.

## 2. Strategic: the summit lost its agenda, and its host is reluctant

Yesterday's evening entry recorded the trilateral as upgraded from announced to scheduled, with both principals confirmed. Today's Reuters analysis (Hunnicutt, Chu, Osborn) puts real substance in what "scheduled" means, and it is less than it looked.

Down-to-the-wire negotiations **have not produced an agenda or a format**. The meeting is expected in Shenzhen around APEC, not confirmed as a structured session. A senior administration official: "we do not expect to get to any kind of agreement" on nuclear issues. Beijing's embassy: "We have nothing to share at the moment."

The three arrive wanting incompatible things. Putin wants an end to the Ukraine war on his terms. Trump wants a legacy photograph and possible movement on arms control. Xi wants **not to be the host of an arms-control conversation at all** — China holds roughly 500 warheads against 5,000+ each for the US and Russia, and to sit at a table framed as three peer nuclear powers is to accept a status Beijing has spent a decade denying. Kagan's line is the operative one: "This is something the Chinese hate." Beijing has also resisted putting Ukraine on the agenda; Moscow sought exactly that; a US official says Trump is unlikely to raise it.

So the correct read is a downgrade in kind, not in likelihood: the meeting will probably happen, and it is a **spectacle with no instrument**. Russia's nationalists are treating the photograph itself as the prize — Dugin: "It shows who is important in the world." That is the tell. A summit whose value is conferred by being photographed is not a summit that produces verification language, and yesterday's entry already recorded that no draft mentions inspectors.

## 3. Enforcement: the record was set the week the voters said it went too far

DHS figures: ICE deported **483,200** people in FY2026 — the highest in the agency's history, 18% above the 2012 Obama-era benchmark and ~78% above FY2024. Arrests exceeded 491,000, including ~475,000 administrative arrests. August alone was 50,925 arrests, a monthly record; September still ran above 48,000.

Then the two-track facts, which are the reason this is a card and not a wire item:

- **The President conceded.** On 60 Minutes: "I think it was too tough." He said he wants to work with Democrats on immigration legislation.
- **The operations accelerated.** He elevated Stephen Miller — the architect of the restrictive policy — to White House chief of staff. Officials say the crackdown will not slow. ICE has been instructed to target ~19,000 arrests per week, a pre-midterm target well above historical pace, and $170bn is already appropriated with $75bn for ICE.
- **The public split.** CBS exit polling: 58% say enforcement went too far, 22% about right, 20% not far enough. Support for border security stays high; discomfort is with interior operations.
- **The counting dispute.** TRAC, using case-level records, finds formal removals were 63% of July–August "deportations"; the rest were voluntary departures and returns. DHS's headline number is not a removals count. That distinction is the whole difference between "record enforcement" and "record paperwork."

This is the cleanest two-track case in the corpus: a rhetorical concession and an operational acceleration in the same fortnight, with the personnel decision pointing to which one is real. It belongs in the enforcement ledger and it should be scored against the divergence, not the volume.

## 4. Courts: the second tool, and the same target

The administration has asked the Supreme Court to curtail the power judges have held since 1946 under the Administrative Procedure Act to "hold unlawful and set aside" an agency rule — nationwide. Its argument: relief should reach only the plaintiffs who sued. Cert is expected to be considered early next year. The case arises from a challenge by Texas medical providers to the "surprise billing" rule; the Fifth Circuit voided parts of it nationwide in August.

The sequence matters. In June 2025 the Court restricted **universal injunctions** in the birthright-citizenship litigation. That left the APA's vacatur power untouched. This appeal is aimed at exactly the remainder — the tool that removes an unlawful rule from the books rather than suspending it against one plaintiff. If it succeeds, Bamzai's description is the consequence: the government can keep enforcing a rule a court has found unlawful, against everyone except the plaintiffs, for years.

Note the alignment that is easy to miss: Texas, Florida and other Republican-led states filed against the administration here, arguing the APA is essential to curb federal overreach and must not be "neutered." The coalition is not partisan; the target is judicial power as such. That is the pattern worth tracking — the same structural move, pursued by whichever side currently benefits, in both directions. Court-order compliance is on the gate calendar for a reason.
