<summary><b>Iran's Revolutionary Guard declares a new offensive doctrine as it threatens strikes on neighbors' airports and pipelines</b></summary>

<b>What happened:</b> Iran's Revolutionary Guard announced that its military doctrine has formally shifted from defense to offense, saying it would consider invading in response to attack. Iranian officials separately warned that if the United States and Israel resume strikes, Iran could hit airports, pipelines and other civilian and economic targets in neighboring countries. President Trump spoke with Vladimir Putin, who urged diplomacy; American officials say a peace deal is "extremely difficult." U.S. officials have reportedly prepared new strikes for after the November 3 election.

<b>Why it matters:</b> A regional war that began as strikes on military sites is now explicitly threatening civilian infrastructure across borders. The timing is also telling: the strike preparation is being reported as scheduled for after the election, meaning the war has become tied to the domestic political calendar.

<b>The pattern:</b> Governments under political pressure at home often hold foreign escalation in reserve until after a vote — the pressure can be aimed outward later, or used as the closing argument. Here the war is being sequenced around Election Day itself.

<b>What history says:</b> Leaders facing declining approval have repeatedly turned to foreign crises in the run-up to elections, from the Falklands in 1982 to Russia's mobilization moves before its 2022 annexation votes. The sequencing is rarely accidental.

<b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether strikes are launched before November 1 — which would break the apparent freeze — or whether the region stays quiet until the votes are counted, and what that silence itself signals.

</details>

<summary><b>Federal prosecutors charge a powerful Virginia state senator 12 days before the election</b></summary>

<b>What happened:</b> The Justice Department indicted Virginia State Senator L. Louise Lucas — the ranking Democrat on the Senate finance committee — along with several relatives, on charges of leading a criminal marijuana-distribution enterprise, including drug trafficking, money laundering and fraud. She surrendered Thursday. Department officials deny the timing is political; the state's governor and Democrats noted the charges came 12 days before Election Day, during Virginia's closest races in years.

<b>Why it matters:</b> Federal prosecutors answer to the administration. When high-profile charges against opposition figures land inside an election's closing window, the line between law enforcement and campaign tooling gets hard to see — regardless of whether the underlying case has merit.

<b>The pattern:</b> Legal charges have a unique power as political weapons: they generate headlines that look neutral and hard to rebut. The question is never just "is the case strong" but "when was it chosen to break."

<b>What history says:</b> In the 1930s Germany and more recently in Turkey and Russia, prosecutors moved against senior opposition figures in the weeks before votes; each case came wrapped in genuine-seeming allegations. Democracies that survived this pattern survived because courts and press held the line on evidence rather than timing.

<b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether campaign advertising built on the indictment appears within days, and whether further charges against state-level Democrats follow before November 3.

</details>

<summary><b>A man attempted to firebomb an ICE office in Atlanta; the device caught on the perimeter fence</b></summary>

<b>What happened:</b> Late Monday, a man threw an improvised incendiary device at the federal building housing ICE's Atlanta field office. It caught on the perimeter fence and no one was hurt. The FBI is offering a $20,000 reward for the suspect. The Department of Homeland Security immediately blamed hostile rhetoric for the attack.

<b>Why it matters:</b> Attacks on enforcement facilities, even failed ones, give a government under political pressure a ready-made narrative. The official framing — that criticism of immigration enforcement causes attacks — converts a crime story into an argument about the administration's opponents in the final stretch of an election.

<b>The pattern:</b> A violent incident followed immediately by a blame-the-opposition framing is one of the oldest crisis-exploitation sequences. The speed and coherence of the official response often matters more than the incident itself.

<b>What history says:</b> The Reichstag fire of 1933 is the canonical case: a single arson attempt produced emergency decrees that suspended civil liberties within 24 hours. Modern examples are less dramatic but structurally similar — incidents harvested for political framing within the news cycle.

<b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether the suspect is identified and attributed a political identity, and whether administration officials use the incident in campaign messaging before November 3.

</details>

<summary><b>Investigation finds hundreds of U.S. citizens have been detained by immigration agents</b></summary>

<b>What happened:</b> A PBS NewsHour investigation documents hundreds of cases of U.S. citizens detained by ICE. Separately, an American citizen in Charlotte said agents pointed a gun at his face and broke his truck windows while detaining him, and an independent analysis reports that deportations have now reached roughly one million — though the share with criminal records remains small, and a former DHS data chief called the White House's departure tallies "highly unlikely."

<b>Why it matters:</b> The enforcement program's public justification is that it targets dangerous criminals. The data emerging says otherwise: the largest deportation operation in modern history is sweeping in citizens and long-term residents at scale, and the government's own numbers don't withstand outside scrutiny.

<b>The pattern:</b> Enforcement systems expand by removing the checks between a person and a decision. First the questioning, then the detention, then the classification — each step becomes easier as the volume grows and the review thins.

<b>What history says:</b> Operations that moved from immigrant communities to the general population — like the Palmer Raids of 1919-20 and Japanese internment in 1942 — each began with a "targeted" mandate that proved elastic under pressure.

<b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether the citizen-detention findings reach broadcast news or stay in print coverage, and whether the courts impose any remedy before the election.

</details>

<summary><b>A federal judge blocks the door-to-door hunt for noncitizen voters</b></summary>

<b>What happened:</b> A federal judge ruled against the Department of Homeland Security's policy of sending agents door-to-door to track down possible noncitizen voters. The ruling came as records revealed ICE reviewed tens of millions of voter files and considered deploying undercover agents.

<b>Why it matters:</b> Voter-file surveillance by immigration agents was an unprecedented use of enforcement power for electoral purposes. The court's block is a check on that expansion — but the underlying data program already exists, and rulings change methods more rarely than they change tools.

<b>The pattern:</b> When a direct approach is blocked, enforcement bureaucracies rarely abandon the objective; they route around the obstacle. Watch what replaces the door-knockers.

<b>What history says:</b> Watergate's core lesson was that surveillance programs are rarely dismantled by court orders alone — they are renamed, reorganized, and re-run under new authority.

<b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether DHS complies in practice, or whether the same voter-file work re-emerges under a different program name before certification season begins.

</details>

<summary><b>Democrats lead national polls by 9 to 11 points with 11 days until the midterms</b></summary>

<b>What happened:</b> A CNN poll finds the president's party in deepening trouble as the economic backdrop worsens. National polls now show Democrats leading on the generic congressional ballot by 9 to 11 points — larger than the same stage in the 2018 and 2006 wave elections. All 435 House seats and 33 Senate seats are on the ballot November 3.

<b>Why it matters:</b> Leads of this size this late have almost never collapsed in modern polling history. The open question is not whether Republicans lose seats, but how many — and whether a Democratic House would be able to check the administration's consolidated power.

<b>The pattern:</b> Midterms are always referendums on the governing party. What's unusual this cycle is the pairing: a large polling lead arriving at the same time as the losing side's campaign is already attacking the polls themselves as fabricated.

<b>What history says:</b> The president's party has lost House seats in every midterm since 2002 except one. When a "fake polls" narrative appears before votes are counted, it has usually functioned as pre-positioning for a dispute over results rather than as analysis.

<b>What we're watching next:</b> Early-voting turnout composition — the one data stream immune to polling error — and whether the polling-attack rhetoric intensifies in the final week.

</details>

<summary><b>France's protest wave spreads abroad as arrests top 2,400</b></summary>

<b>What happened:</b> France's protest wave — triggered by €54 billion in budget cuts and the high cost of living — continues into its second month, with more than 2,400 arrests and the worst street violence in decades. The wave is now crossing borders: Britain's Green Party is explicitly urging people "inspired by protests in France" to join an anti-austerity rally in London, while French political commentators argue about whether the country can afford the unrest.

<b>Why it matters:</b> A protest wave that spreads across borders converts a national budget fight into a continental mood shift. Markets are watching France's debt crisis nervously; if austerity politics become unpassable across Europe, the Continent's fiscal constraint story changes fundamentally.

<b>The pattern:</b> Economic squeeze produces street movements; street movements produce either capture by a party or suppression by the state — and the direction a government chooses tells you more than the protests do.

<b>What history says:</b> France's own cycle of street-driven crisis — 1848, 1968 — shows unrest rarely resolves the underlying fiscal question; it reorganizes who gets to decide it.

<b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether the movement gets adopted by an established French political force, or whether the government pushes emergency measures instead.

</details>

<summary><b>U.S. greenhouse emissions rose last year as coal use increased, U.N. report finds</b></summary>

<b>What happened:</b> A new U.N. report shows U.S. greenhouse gas emissions increased last year, driven largely by more coal burning. Meanwhile the Supreme Court keeps full SNAP food payments on hold as a government shutdown deal nears.

<b>Why it matters:</b> The emissions rise runs against every announced decarbonization commitment and shows how energy economics — cheap domestic coal — can overwhelm policy frameworks in a crisis period. Paired with benefits being held hostage in shutdown bargaining, it captures the squeeze pattern: costs moving onto households while policy moves away from them.

<b>The pattern:</b> In periods of institutional strain, environmental and social-welfare commitments are the first casualties because they require future-oriented state capacity — exactly what crisis governments stop having.

<b>What history says:</b> The 1930s and 1980s both saw energy policy override environmental commitments when economic pressure peaked; the reversal only ended when the underlying economy recovered.

<b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether the shutdown deal preserves SNAP benefits, and whether emissions trends change with the next energy pricing cycle.
