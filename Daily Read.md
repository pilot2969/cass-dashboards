<p><b>What history says:</b> In the 1930s Reichstag-era playbook, the machinery of justice was repurposed against opposition leaders in the run-up to elections; the lesson recorded across failed democracies is that a party that wins while jailing opponents tends not to need to win again the same way.</p>

<summary>The Pentagon lends a 'heat ray' to the border police</summary>

<p><b>What happened:</b> The Defense Department has authorized Homeland Security to test a military 'heat ray' — the Active Denial System, a weapon that fires an invisible beam causing intense burning skin pain at ranges beyond half a mile — for possible use against migrants at the southern border. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth signed the memo in September, lending one system to DHS for seven months. The weapon was trialed in Afghanistan two decades ago and never used in combat.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters:</b> This crosses a line the US military has held for decades: a crowd-control weapon designed for war zones was never used on people, and now it is being adapted for use against civilians — specifically migrants — on US soil. 'Testing' is the standard first step between authorization and deployment.</p>

<p><b>The pattern:</b> Security apparatuses expand by testing on the least protected populations first. What is trialed on migrants at the border does not stay at the border: protest policing, prison control, and crowd management are the documented downstream users.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> Every institutionalized repression system on record normalized its tools through border or custodial populations before applying them domestically at scale. The language of 'non-lethal' does the work of making the intolerable administratively acceptable.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether the system is deployed against gatherings (border crossings, protests) rather than only in open terrain; any injury reports from the testing period; and whether DHS formalizes a permanent transfer after the seven-month loan.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters:</b> One fabricated poll doesn't just mislead; it moved real money and ended a real campaign. The difference — Wedgewood's fakes favored the left — will not survive the retelling.</p>

<p><b>The pattern:</b> Trust infrastructure destroyed for one faction's purposes is destroyed for everyone's. An information ecosystem where fabricated polls move markets and campaigns cannot be repaired by 'better polling' — it can only be repaired by provenance: who made a number, and can it be checked.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> Disinformation campaigns historically peak in the two weeks before a vote, when there is no time for corrections to circulate. The tell to watch is not whether the story is covered, but whether it gets fused to the pre-existing 'the polls are rigged' frame.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether the Wedgewood admission is absorbed into the fake-polls fraud narrative this week; whether other fabricated polls surface before November 3; and whether polling outlets adopt provenance standards (registration, methodology disclosure) in response — or decide the industry is unsalvageable.</p>

The security framing used here — foreign influence embedded in domestic institutions — is the same framing that could later be applied to bodies with far more reach: diaspora political organizations, internationally connected religious institutions, civic organizing groups.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> Previous escalations in domestic-targeting policy have followed a ladder: symbolic designation first, then enforcement against funding, then legal action against the institutions themselves. Each step was tested on a small case before being applied broadly.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Whether the next designation targets a living institution — one with an active constituency — rather than a nearly extinct program. That would signal the ladder has moved to its next rung.</p>

<p>The Justice Department filed a federal lawsuit asking a judge to stop Alaska from certifying its Senate election if Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan loses to Democrat Mary Peltola but "would have won" counting votes cast for a second candidate also named Daniel J. Sullivan. The suit argues "voter confusion" could tip one of the closest races in the country. Asked about it Friday, Trump said plainly: "I'm the one that said do it."</p>

<p><b>Why it matters:</b> This is the first time a president has personally claimed credit for a pre-election lawsuit to block certification of a Senate race. The suit was filed 11 days before Election Day, before any votes could be miscounted.</p>

<p><b>The pattern:</b> The federal government is normally the party that certifies results stand unless a loser proves fraud after the fact. Here, the Justice Department asks a court to pre-bank an order that would stop certification before the result is even known — reversing the burden of proof ahead of time.</p>

<p><b>History says:</b> Election litigation in past cycles came after votes were counted, by losing campaigns. Governments have pre-cleared legal instruments for contested elections in weaker democracies — the legal action is prepared in advance so it can be used the moment it is needed.</p>

<p><b>Watching next:</b> Whether the Alaska court acts before November 3, whether similar suits appear in other close states, and whether the second Sullivan candidate — who denies coordinating — is ever traced to a sponsor.</p>

<p><b>History says:</b> Accuracy concerns are best handled before ballots are mailed, not by singling out tens of thousands of ballots for exclusion after they are cast. Historically, vague lists of "suspect voters" have skewed heavily against naturalized citizens and minority communities.</p>

<p><b>Watching next:</b> Whether courts act before November 3, whether California slows or changes its counting timeline, and whether the "doomsday scenario" language reappears as a standing justification.</p>

<summary>Explainer: what a "color revolution" is — and why it's suddenly everywhere on the right</summary>

<p><b>What happened:</b> In the past two days, former national security adviser Michael Flynn called for the arrest of former officials who took part in a nonprofit planning exercise, calling them "traitors." The material fueling the push traces to Jennica Pounds — the online figure "DataRepublican" — who has described what she calls "color revolution" tactics inside the United States. Pounds is not just an online commentator: the Defense Department confirmed she has held a special-government-employee post since July. Heritage Foundation's oversight project director Mike Howell described the country as drifting toward a "cold civil war," and Senator Mike Lee amplified the claims while saying he hoped the recordings were a deepfake. Thirteen days before the election, the language of foreign-backed subversion is being pointed at American civic groups and former officials.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters:</b> "Color revolution" is a real term of art from the 2000s, naming popular uprisings — Serbia's bulldozer revolution, Georgia's Rose revolution, Ukraine's Orange revolution — in which mass civic movements, often backed by Western funding and training, toppled entrenched governments. Authoritarian governments from Moscow to Tehran have long used the phrase as a catch-all accusation: any domestic protest, they claim, is not real grievance but a foreign-directed script. That same vocabulary is now being applied inside the United States — with the accusers in power.</p>

<p><b>The pattern:</b> The language does specific work. First, it converts citizens into agents: a protest is not people angry about something, it is the opening move of a foreign-style plot. Second, it converts preparation into conspiracy: the targeted materials here were scenario exercises — groups game-planning what would happen if an election were contested or court orders ignored — and planning for a constitutional crisis is being recast as plotting one. Third, it prepares arrests before any crime: calling people "traitors" who should be "found and arrested" pre-frames future prosecutions as counterintelligence, not politics. And there is an inversion at the center: the leaked "illegitimacy criteria" document at issue defines an election as illegitimate if, say, a government defies court orders or turns force on voters — conditions that describe the administration's own documented conduct more closely than anyone else's.</p>

<p><b>What history says:</b> Accusing the opposition of foreign-directed subversion is the oldest move in the authoritarian playbook — the Reichstag fire was blamed on communists within weeks of the 1933 emergency decree; decades of crackdowns followed the same script. What is newer is the pipeline: a government-adjacent researcher's claims flow through political figures with institutional standing (a senator, a former national security adviser, a foundation's oversight arm) and emerge sounding like an official finding. That laundering — fringe claim to authoritative voice — is how pre-justification is built before any enforcement happens.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next:</b> Four tells. Whether the Justice Department opens any investigation into the named groups or former officials — that would turn the rhetoric into an instrument. Whether "traitor" language migrates from personal social media into White House or DOJ official channels. Whether the administration adopts the "color revolution" frame itself to describe domestic opposition. And whether Pounds's government post is used to present her material as government-sourced. If the recordings at the center of this turn out to be edited or fabricated, the story changes — but the push happened either way.</p>
