<summary>The war has a start date, and it's the day after the vote</summary>

<p><b>What happened.</b> Iranian negotiators left the talks in Doha on Sunday without an agreement. A draft proposal is now being circulated and a follow-up meeting is possible. Iran's foreign minister calls it a "two-day plan" to reopen the Strait of Hormuz with no final conclusion yet. Over the same weekend, Axios reported that Israel's prime minister pressed President Trump in a phone call to strike Iran <i>immediately after</i> Tuesday's midterms, while Trump said he wants to let negotiations play out. Trump had promised no new attacks before the vote. He has not promised anything after it.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> For months the restraint on this war has been explained as strategy. The calendar suggests it was scheduling. When the constraint that produced a pause is an election rather than a deal, the pause ends when the polls close — and the two sides spent the last three days saying opposite things about what happens next, each to their own audience. Iran's foreign ministry told its public on Monday that no American attack is coming. That is either intelligence or advertising.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> Negotiations and attacks are running on separate clocks. In the same week that Doha was talking, a Qatari gas carrier called the <i>Al Mayeda</i> — bound for Pakistan, and cleared for passage by Iran itself — was struck by unidentified objects near Hormuz. Two more projectiles hit a tanker off eastern Saudi Arabia on Monday morning. Qatar condemned the attack and reserved the right to respond, which is an awkward position for the country hosting the mediation.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> Deadline diplomacy under an electoral clock usually fails for a structural reason rather than a diplomatic one: the side that would have to concede gains nothing by conceding before the vote, and the side that wants a strike gains nothing by striking before it. Talks that recess days before an election and resume days after it are not necessarily failing talks. They are talks taking the week off.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether anything lands between now and the close of polls on Tuesday night. Whether the Doha draft turns into a published text or a date for reopening the strait by 10 November. And whether the tankers photographed at an air base in the Negev on Sunday turn out to have been a staging signal rather than a stock photograph.</p>

<p><b>Sources.</b> Reuters; Axios via Arab News and Al Jazeera; UK Maritime Trade Operations; Qatar Ministry of Foreign Affairs; Bloomberg via Pakistan Today.</p>

<summary>Three courts said no, and the plan went ahead anyway</summary>

<p><b>What happened.</b> A federal judge dismissed the Justice Department's lawsuit against Alaska on Sunday, ruling that the United States has no recognised interest in which candidate wins a particular election. On Thursday a federal appeals court rejected the administration's second attempt to force California to set aside ballots from 20,000 people it calls "likely noncitizens" — the third court loss on that effort in two weeks, with the majority saying the emergency was "at least part of the government's own making." No appeal to the Supreme Court had been confirmed as of Monday morning, though one is expected. The deployment of roughly 1,000 federal election observers is going ahead regardless.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> Since October the administration has pursued three routes to contest the midterms: litigation in the states, federal observers on the ground, and pre-built claims about the result. The litigation route is now failing in every forum it has been tried, and failing on the same ground — that the federal government doesn't get to pick winners. That leaves two routes that don't require a judge.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> This is what it looks like when a strategy's legal leg is amputated while the operational legs keep walking. Nobody has been ordered to stand down. No filing has been withdrawn. The observers still deploy, the press offices still brief, and the claims that will be tested on Tuesday night were written before the courts spoke. Losing in court costs a lawsuit. The message it sent to voters is free.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> Contested elections in the United States have rarely turned on whether the challenge was legally sound. In 1876 the dispute was resolved by a commission whose authority was invented for the occasion. In 2000 it was resolved by a 5-4 vote that stopped a count. What mattered each time was whether enough institutions were willing to treat a loss as a loss. Court losses do not stop a contestation. They tell you who is still willing to say yes.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether an emergency application reaches the Supreme Court on Monday and how narrow it is. Whether any state official declines to certify on a schedule. And whether a concession lands within 24 hours of Tuesday's result, which is the first thing the "no crisis" control case actually tests.</p>

<p><b>Sources.</b> Democracy Docket; CBS News; Alaska Public Media; NPR.</p>

<summary>The oil number coming from the Treasury isn't measured, it's asserted</summary>

<p><b>What happened.</b> Brent crude fell about 3% on Monday to roughly $110 a barrel, down from a four-year high of $116.72 on Friday, and West Texas Intermediate dropped to about $101. OPEC+ held its December production targets. The fall was driven by diplomacy, not by supply: the Treasury Secretary posted on Saturday that transit through the Strait of Hormuz is "back to pre-conflict levels minus Iranian barrels," adding that "the world can live without Iranian oil."</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> The operational picture doesn't match the claim. US Central Command's own tally of vessels turned away stands at 144. An independent tracker logged the strait as critical on day 242, with 23 attacks on shipping since 1 October and roughly 492 vessels waiting to transit. Liquefied natural gas flows through Hormuz ran at about 30% of pre-war volumes in October. A treasury statement that moves a 3-4% oil tape while the shipping data says the waterway is still contested is not a measurement of the situation. It is a bid to shape it.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> Watch the direction of the claim, not the honesty of it. When an official number is offered to calm a market, the interesting question is what it was designed to move. The answer here was a war premium that had become an electoral liability, on the eve of a vote where prices are the most-cited grievance. The number did its job on the tape. The tankers did not move.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> Official statements about a contested waterway have a poor record as forecasts and a good record as instruments. In 1987, during the Tanker War, assurances that the Gulf was under control ran alongside reflagging operations, escort convoys and mine strikes. The claim and the reality can both be true statements about different things — one about intent, the other about a strait with 492 ships waiting outside it.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether the attack rate on shipping falls over the next week, which would validate the claim, or holds at something like a strike every day and a half, which would falsify it. Whether Brent holds above $108 through the vote. And whether the Qataris respond to the <i>Al Mayeda</i> strike while still chairing the talks.</p>

<p><b>Sources.</b> Treasury Secretary on X; Benzinga; AGBI; UK Maritime Trade Operations; Gulf European Forum tracker; Bloomberg via Pakistan Today.</p>

<summary>Michigan ended the unity experiment two days before the vote</summary>

<p><b>What happened.</b> Representative Haley Stevens endorsed the Republican Senate candidate, Mike Rogers, finishing a public split with her own party two days after a scheduled unity event was called off. The President said he would "love" for her to join the Republican Party. Separately, the final New York Times/Siena battleground survey has Democrats ahead in five of seven key Senate races, with no clear leader in the other two.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> Michigan was one of the states the administration's midterm strategy was built around: a Democratic coalition expected to fracture over the war, and a Senate seat that would follow. The fracture happened — it just happened in the opposite direction from the one that was predicted, with a sitting Democrat backing the Republican and a Republican president inviting her across the aisle rather than the reverse.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> Coalition fractures are usually read as a leading indicator of a wave. They are a leading indicator of <i>movement</i>. What matters is which side of the fracture holds the voters, and the polling suggests the movement is running against the party in power — five of seven battleground Senate races leaning away, with disapproval of the President at 60% in an NBC survey and prices tied to the war cited as the reason.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> Midterm waves are rarely produced by a single defection and almost never by a defection that gets cheered by the other party. They are produced by the cost of a war becoming a kitchen-table number. The 2006 wave was built on Iraq as a monthly figure. The 1950 midterms were built on the same thing in Korea. When prices at a pump are the top grievance and the war is the reason for the prices, the election is a referendum on the war whether or not anyone campaigns that way.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether a second Democratic endorser follows within 24 hours, which would indicate a coordinated move rather than a personal one. Whether the President's post-vote decision on Iran is read by voters as the thing this election was about.</p>

<p><b>Sources.</b> AP; CBS News; CNN; Al Jazeera.</p>

<summary>Poland's court fight found an outside referee and no way out</summary>

<p><b>What happened.</b> The European Commission said the appointment of eight members of Poland's Constitutional Tribunal "lies within the constitutional power of the Sejm," citing a Venice Commission opinion and European Court of Human Rights interim measures from May. Prime Minister Donald Tusk ruled out any compromise in a televised address on Saturday. President Karol Nawrocki has refused to swear the judges in and calls the situation a breach of the constitutional order. The justice minister says the Tribunal will resume work within weeks, and police remain deployed at the building.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> The Commission's intervention supplies the one thing a constitutional crisis of this shape never has: an external arbiter with a written opinion. It also doesn't settle anything, because the dispute is not about what the law says. It is about who is entitled to say it.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> Poland's crisis and the American one share a mechanism. In each, a court's composition is contested by a political actor, and the claim is procedural — who was appointed correctly — while the stakes are about electoral outcomes the court will adjudicate. External validation helps a government win an argument. It cannot make an institution legitimate in the eyes of people who already withdrew consent.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> Constitutional courts that lose the argument to a president or a party tend to lose it permanently, regardless of who is legally right. Hungary's court was turned by expansion and appointment rather than by ruling. Poland's has been fought over for a decade, through two governments, and its independence rating has been an EU funding condition the entire time. The institution matters less than whether the losing side accepted the loss the first time.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether the Tribunal issues a ruling with judges sworn by only one side of the dispute, and whether the government recognises it. Whether the €137 billion in frozen EU funds gets tied to this. Whether the standoff stays in the courtroom or moves to the street.</p>

<p><b>Sources.</b> POLITICO Europe; Euronews; CNN; Kyiv Independent.</p>
