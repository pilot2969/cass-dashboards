<summary>[09:15] Five cabinet members are living on military bases, and the Iran threat does not explain it</summary>

<p><i>The Treasury Secretary moving onto a base was reported as one man's security detail. It is five or six senior civilians living inside the armed forces' housing.</i></p>

<p><b>What happened.</b> The wire reported that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent moved to a military installation after Iranian threats. He is not alone. The Secretary of State and the Secretary of Defense both live on "Generals' Row" at Fort McNair in Washington — Hegseth in Quarters 8, the house normally reserved for the Army's vice chief of staff, at $4,655 a month, according to Pentagon figures reported by the New York Times. The Homeland Security Secretary moved into the Coast Guard commandant's residence after her home address was published. The deputy chief of staff and the Attorney General are also in military housing.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> An Iranian threat explains guarding one man. It does not explain why the civilians who oversee the military have moved into the military's own housing stock, in houses built for generals, on a permanent footing. Protection is not the same as tenancy. The people who are supposed to hold the armed forces accountable now depend on them for a roof, and the officers whose quarters they occupy have been displaced.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> This sits inside a Pentagon programme, on the record since October, to make every domestic base self-sufficient for 90 days: its own power, fuel, water and communications. Installation hardening is one part of it. Senior officials living inside the hardened perimeter is the same programme seen from the other end. A house on a base that can run itself for three months is not a safe house. It is a place where government can keep operating.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> The closest precedent is September 2001, when senior officials were moved to secret alternate sites for days. That was brief, undisclosed and limited to a handful of people. This is months long, openly reported, and covers the civilian chain of command rather than the presidency. There is no precedent for a Treasury Secretary living on an Army post.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether they move back out when the Iran crisis recedes. If the housing was contingency, it reverses. If it was structural, it does not — and persistence alone no longer settles the question, because a base built to sustain itself gives officials a reason to stay that has nothing to do with any threat.</p>

<summary>[09:05] The White House told Congress that investigating it could be a crime</summary>

<p><i>A letter to the incoming House Judiciary chairman does not say Congress lacks the power to investigate. It says exercising it could be criminal.</i></p>

<p><b>What happened.</b> Punchbowl News reported a letter from White House counsel David Scharf to Rep. Jamie Garcia, who takes the Judiciary gavel in January, warning that some planned investigations "could cross the line into blatant criminality." The letter restates the settled rule that Congress may only investigate for a legislative purpose — a limit Democrats have invoked themselves. What is new is the second half: attaching criminal exposure to oversight that goes past it.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> Congress has subpoena power because courts have enforced it for a century. A committee can compel documents and testimony, and refusal carries contempt. If the administration answers a subpoena by saying the committee's motive may be a crime, the threat is not aimed at the committee. It is aimed at every witness deciding whether to appear, and every member deciding whether the subpoenas will hold.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> Third time in five weeks that a function of Congress has been re-described as an injury to a person. In October the Pentagon routed oversight of commanders back through commanders. Last week the Justice Department described itself as the President's personal legal representative in a case about the chief of staff. Now oversight itself is priced as prosecution. The form is the same each time: a shared rule becomes asymmetric the moment one side can charge for it.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> Every administration tests the legislative-purpose line; Congress has stretched it for a decade and mostly won. What is unusual is not the boundary, it is the penalty attached to crossing it. Contempt of Congress is a process. A criminal referral is a threat, and threats change behaviour before any case is filed.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether "criminality" means a contempt citation, a referral to the Justice Department, or a warning about obstructing an investigation. Each is a different threat level, and the letter's full text has not been published. Also whether the first subpoena of the new majority is answered with documents or with a lawyer's letter.</p>

<summary>[08:55] The House killed the impeachment without voting on it</summary>

<p><i>211 to 204 to send the charges to committee. Nobody voted on whether the secretary should stay.</i></p>

<p><b>What happened.</b> The House voted 211–204 on Tuesday to refer Rep. Thomas Massie's impeachment resolution against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to the Judiciary Committee, effectively ending the effort. Every Republican but Massie voted for referral; 11 Democrats were absent. The President called the result "an EXONERATION of a Great American Patriot." The resolution was privileged, which forced the vote within two legislative days of filing.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> No member has yet voted on whether the conduct described in the eight articles is acceptable. The one vote taken was on where the paperwork goes. That is a real procedural win for the administration and a real answer to nothing: the charges — the Iran operation, the detention orders, the restriction of a senator's speech — remain on the record, unexamined and unrefuted. A referral is also not permanent. It is a decision about this Congress's attention, and attention is the thing majorities control.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> When a privileged measure is inconvenient, the disposal is jurisdictional rather than substantive. You do not defend the conduct; you move the question to a committee whose calendar you write. The tell is the vote count: a party that believed the charges were baseless would have welcomed a vote on the merits and won it. Instead it spent its one guaranteed floor vote on the off-ramp.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> Referral has ended most impeachment attempts in American history, and it has rarely ended the argument. The 1974 Judiciary Committee began as a referral. The 2019 Ukraine inquiry moved through exactly this gate twice. The mechanism that revives a buried resolution is never a better speech; it is a new fact — a document, a witness, a transcript — that makes the committee's inaction cost more than its action. Absent one, referral holds.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether Judiciary schedules anything at all, and whether the White House keeps using the word exoneration once it is clear no exoneration was voted on. A claim repeated past its evidence is the kind of thing that gets tested again in a different chamber.</p>

<summary>[08:50] Sanders and Slotkin refuse to back Schumer, the morning after the sweep</summary>

<p><i>The party just won the Senate. Its leadership vote is now contested.</i></p>

<p><b>What happened.</b> Sen. Bernie Sanders said Tuesday he will not support Chuck Schumer for Senate Democratic leader in the new Congress, saying "the time is now for change." Sen. Elissa Slotkin joined him the same day. The objections came roughly a week after Democrats' midterm sweep handed them the majority.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> A leadership challenge launched immediately after a victory is unusual, and it is the opposite of the normal sequence, in which a losing leader is blamed and a winning one is credited. That inversion is the substance here: Sanders and Slotkin are not arguing Schumer lost seats, they are arguing the win was delivered despite him — by candidates running to his left on economic questions, in states where the national party brand was a liability. Whoever leads the new majority decides what the majority is for.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> Coalitions that expand fast develop a legitimacy gap at the top. The leadership was selected by a smaller, more moderate caucus; the new senators were elected by an electorate that wanted something else. When that gap opens, the fight is never about competence. It is about which voters the party thinks it now answers to.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> Democrats have done this twice in living memory — the 2016 and 2024 post-loss leadership fights — and both times the challenge failed because the incumbent controlled the institutional levers and the challenger had no alternative coalition. The difference now is that the challengers are not asking for a post-mortem. They are asking for a vote while the mandate is fresh, and they can point at a result.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether this becomes an actual contested ballot or stays a series of statements. A private vote with two declared opponents is a different event from two senators talking to cameras. Watch for a third name being floated as a compromise — that is what a leadership fight looks like when it is real.</p>

<summary>[08:45] Senate Republicans keep Thune, and keep the argument about why they lost</summary>

<p><i>Facing the minority for the first time in four years, the conference chose continuity.</i></p>

<p><b>What happened.</b> Senate Republicans reelected John Thune as their leader on Tuesday after a day of private discussion about steep midterm losses that cost them the majority. Some colleagues had pushed to delay the leadership election; that effort failed.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> The move to postpone was the more revealing event. A conference that believes it lost for reasons it can name holds its election on schedule. A conference that cannot name the reason tries to buy time. Thune survives, which means Senate Republicans have decided the losses were not his to own — and have left the diagnosis of 2026 to whoever writes the minority's messaging.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> Parties that lose power rarely remove their leader; they remove the leader a cycle later, once the losses can be attributed. The exception is a decisive defeat with a clear author. This was decisive and has no author, which is the worst combination for accountability: everyone can see the result and nobody can be blamed for it.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> Senate Republicans have replaced their leader four times since 1980, and only once — 2002, Lott — under direct pressure from outside the chamber. The conference is the most continuity-prone body in Washington. Thune's position is safe until the minority's first real defeat, at which point the question of what the party is for arrives all at once.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether the conference produces a post-mortem document or lets the diagnosis stay informal. The document is the tell: an official account of why you lost is a commitment about what you will do differently.</p>

<summary>[08:40] Seven in ten Americans want this Congress to end the Iran war</summary>

<p><i>Half of Republicans agree. The war has lost the electorate it was launched under.</i></p>

<p><b>What happened.</b> A four-day Reuters/Ipsos poll completed Monday found 71% of Americans want Congress to pass legislation ending the conflict with Iran — 93% of Democrats and 51% of Republicans. Trump launched the war with Israel in February.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> The war is not a fringe objection. It is the majority position, including among the President's own voters, and it is being reported the same week the House disposed of an impeachment resolution whose central charge was that war. Members have now been asked to protect a secretary over a policy most of their constituents want ended — and the machinery available to end it, the appropriations calendar, has not yet been used.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> When a signature policy loses both the electorate and the institution, the fight moves to procedure. Polling creates permission; only a vote creates a constraint. The gap between 71% and no vote is where a war continues to be funded.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> Public opinion did not end Vietnam — the appropriations calendar did. It did not end Iraq — a status-of-forces deadline and a new majority did. The mechanism is always the same and it is never a poll: a vote that stops paying for it.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether any member offers a war-powers resolution or a funding amendment now that a majority party exists with a mandate to do it. An impeachment of one man was survivable. A vote on money is not avoidable indefinitely.</p>

<summary>[08:35] Jordan takes the minority, and the oversight war starts before the gavels do</summary>

<p><i>A 106–73 secret ballot puts the party's most experienced investigator in the one job that still has power.</i></p>

<p><b>What happened.</b> House Republicans elected Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio minority leader on a 106–73 secret ballot, closing a two-candidate field against Rep. Steve Scalise. Jordan ran as "battle-tested on the oversight front." The result was read as a clean break from the Johnson political operation.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> With a Democratic majority of roughly 259 seats and subpoena power, a minority of about 176 has almost no legislative function. What remains is investigation and messaging — and Jordan is the conference's most experienced counter-investigator, having lost three floor Speaker votes and now placed in the only senior job that uses his actual skill at a level he can actually reach. That is not a reward. It is a deployment.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> A party that cannot legislate converts its remaining institutional tools into an inquiry shop. Watch what the minority's first subpoenas target: the outgoing administration's conduct, or the incoming majority's legitimacy. The difference decides whether oversight is a governing function or a campaign.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> Minority leaders with investigative backgrounds — Joe McCarthy's committee, the 1990s House Oversight under Dan Burton — treated the subpoena as a megaphone, and it cost them public standing while consuming the majority's calendar. The successful version, the 2019 and 2021 select committees, picked one thread and subpoenaed in one direction.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> What the first Jordan subpoena is about. Election administration would mean he is preparing a legitimacy case for 2028; Iran or the impeachment files would mean the oversight is defensive.</p>
