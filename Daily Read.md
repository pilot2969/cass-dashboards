<summary>[09:20] Wall Street's best day after a midterm in 44 years, and the oil number is the one that means something</summary>

<p><b>What happened.</b> The Dow closed up 1,569.40 points, 3.03 percent, at 53,356.12 on Wednesday — its best day since April 2025 and its biggest single-day gain after a midterm election since 1982, according to CFRA. The S&amp;P 500 rose 1.58 percent, the Nasdaq 1.44 percent. West Texas Intermediate crude fell more than 8 percent, settling at its lowest level since 31 August. Brent, below $100 for the first time in weeks, was steady to slightly higher Thursday morning near $95 to $96.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> A three percent equity day after an election can be about many things — divided government, the end of uncertainty, the tech trade. An eight percent one-day fall in crude is narrower and more specific: traders marked down, in one session, the probability that the Strait of Hormuz closes and American strikes resume. For six weeks they had been told by both governments, on the record, that the strikes were coming after 3 November. They repriced the day after voters closed the window.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> Markets are not opinions and they are frequently wrong, but this is the first number in this story that is neither a statement by an interested party nor a reading of one. It is a third party putting money on what the president will do next. It is also a price on something larger: a president who cannot legislate and will be constrained from escalating gets priced as a lame duck, not as a crisis. What the tape did on Wednesday was the first independent evidence for that, and it did not come from anything the president said.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> Post-midterm rallies are the rule rather than the exception, and the biggest ones in the 1982 and 1994 cycles were followed by months of ordinary volatility. Crash-less is not the same as stable: the 1982 comparison day was the start of a two-year bull market, the 1994 one preceded a government shutdown fight. The specific claim here is only about oil.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether crude keeps falling or holds. Thursday's bounce of about 1.6 percent is a market discovering that de-escalation and a deal are different things: Eurasia Group cut its odds of an Iran deal to 10 percent the same morning oil fell.</p>

<summary>[09:15] Iran says mines destroyed four tankers in Hormuz. No one else has confirmed it.</summary>

<p><b>What happened.</b> Iran's Revolutionary Guard said on Wednesday that four oil tankers were destroyed after striking naval mines while trying to cross a route it described as prohibited, south of the Strait of Hormuz, and that two other vessels were detained. Overnight into Thursday it added two tankers and a tug. No flag state, shipowner, coalition naval command or insurer has corroborated any of it.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> Whether the ships exist matters less than who is setting the tempo. A mine needs no decision in Washington and no order from anyone the United States talks to. So the American half of this war can pause — and it has, for three days — while the Iranian half continues and the cost of American restraint keeps rising. A president who chooses to de-escalate does not thereby get a quiet strait. He gets a theatre where the other side moves first.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> The word to notice is "prohibited." A route the IRGC declares off-limits is a claim to control the channel, asserted unilaterally and enforced by a weapon nobody can attribute cheaply. If it stands, it is a blockade in everything but name, run by an organisation rather than a navy, at a cost measured in hulls.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> The 1980s Tanker War ran the same way: mines and deniable attacks that pulled a superpower into escorting reflagged tankers rather than into declaring a war. Iran has run that play against two American presidents, forty years apart.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> A hull name, a position, or a war-risk premium. Any one of the three turns a claim into a fact. If a week passes with none, the correct reading is that the claim was the product, not the reporting.</p>

<summary>[09:10] Every tool the president reached for, he had already named before the vote</summary>

<p><b>What happened.</b> Three of the administration's post-election postures turn out to have been declared before polls closed. The veto: "I have the ultimate power — it's called 'veto,'" in a CBS News interview conducted before the vote, where he welcomed the nickname "Mr. Veto." Iran: "no immediate plans" to strike, plus "locked and loaded," in a six-minute Financial Times call on Tuesday, hours before the polls closed. Pardons: a preemptive-pardon float naming Elon Musk and White House staff, made on 2 November.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> The obvious story is that the president lost, collapsed, and reached for whatever tools were left. The chronology does not support it. He had the tools out already. What the defeat did was remove the reason each had been held in reserve — bills worth passing are why vetoes get held, an election calendar is why strikes get deferred, and pardons are cheap to save and expensive to spend.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> That is a weaker leader in a stronger position than "collapse" implies. Weaker, because the initiative is gone. Stronger, because there is nothing improvised to disrupt: a man executing a plan he already wrote does not need to be in charge of events to keep executing it. It also means the two readings of the last two weeks — the horse that rides in, and the landlord counting assets — are not rivals. Both describe a man working from a shorter list.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> Pre-commitment is the norm at the end of a presidency, and it is usually read backwards as reaction. Second-term presidents consistently announce the posture before the electorate decides, then execute it after, because the announcement is itself directed at the electorate. Buchanan in 1860 and Johnson in 1868 both spent their final months running plans they had described before the loss.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> A tool he did not flag before 3 November. Vetoes in the categories he named, pardons for the people he named, and no new war all fit this reading. Anything unannounced would be adaptation, and would count against it.</p>

<summary>[09:05] Republicans expect their own impeachment vote to pass. The target is the defense secretary.</summary>

<p><b>What happened.</b> Thomas Massie will bring a privileged resolution to impeach Pete Hegseth to the floor when the House returns Monday. Multiple Republican sources told CNN they expect it to pass. Trump publicly backed Hegseth on Wednesday — "I think he's doing a very good job" — while CBS reported he is asking aides whether Hegseth should be replaced and floating Governor Ron DeSantis for the Pentagon. Vice President JD Vance said plainly: "the reason we lost is the war." Senator Thom Tillis called his own 2025 confirmation vote the "biggest regret of my career."</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> Two hundred and eighteen votes impeach. Democrats hold 214 seats in the sitting House. A handful of Republicans carries it. Massie lost his primary in May, so he has nothing to protect and no future campaign to think about — which is precisely why he holds the lever. The vote is expected in a House the voters have already fired.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> The Senate will not convict: 67 votes are needed and Democrats will hold at most 56. That was never the mechanism. Resignation is, and a floor vote is the instrument that produces one. What is being built is not a removal but a way out that the party can describe as forced on it. Publicly the president shields him; privately, per CBS, he is shopping for a replacement.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> The House has impeached a cabinet secretary twice, in 1876 and in 2024, and the Senate acquitted both times. Neither vote removed anyone. Both made the office untenable, and in Belknap's case he resigned before the trial.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether anyone else signs on before Monday, and whether the White House finds a name. A resignation before the gavel is the clean read; a resignation after a passing vote is the party choosing to be seen pushing him.</p>

<summary>[09:00] Texas Republicans move to take the powers of the office they just lost</summary>

<p><b>What happened.</b> Texas Senate Republicans meet Thursday afternoon to discuss reorganising their chamber: electing a majority leader, and moving the lieutenant governor's authority over committees, bill referral and the floor calendar into that office. Vikki Goodwin beat Dan Patrick on Tuesday and will preside over the Senate as president of the body, as the state constitution provides. Republicans kept the chamber 19 to 12.</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> In Texas most of the lieutenant governor's power is not in the constitution — it is delegated by the chamber's own rules, which senators adopt at the start of each session. A majority can take it back without contesting a vote, filing a suit, or alleging fraud. There is a constitutional floor: the lieutenant governor presides. So the likely product is not a stripped office but a hollowed one — a presiding officer with a gavel and no calendar.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> This is the cheapest available challenge to an election anywhere in the country. It costs no claim about the count, no litigation, no official's exposure, and it is done by a chamber the same voters handed to the same party. The election stands and the office becomes furniture.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> North Carolina did it to a governor's powers in 2016 and Wisconsin in 2018, both in lame-duck sessions after a loss. Neither reversed an election. Both slowed an incoming administration. In each case voters had another chance at the ballot, and in both states the fight outlasted the session — which is the relevant warning here, because it turns a one-afternoon rules vote into a permanent campaign issue.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether the caucus vote is written into January's rules or quietly dropped, and whether the same bill language appears in a second state within 30 days.</p>

<summary>[08:55] The new majority says it will investigate before it legislates</summary>

<p><b>What happened.</b> Senator Chuck Schumer, on track to lead a Democratic Senate in January, said Wednesday: "whenever Trump defies the will of the American people or the law, we will launch investigations, take him to court, and entangle him in legislative razor wire." He added that "the days of Trump's supplicant Senate are over." Senator Ron Wyden's response to the same question was one word: "Subpoenas."</p>

<p><b>Why it matters.</b> The two instruments are not equivalent. A bill meets a veto and needs two thirds to survive it, which the new majority does not have. A subpoena meets a court fight it can win on a calendar. The cheapest instrument in the building is oversight, and both parties have now discovered that at the same time.</p>

<p><b>The pattern.</b> Add it to Texas: a party that cannot pass anything has every incentive to reach for process. The right has the rules inside one chamber; the left has the subpoena from January. Nobody with capacity reached for a vote count, a fraud claim or a street.</p>

<p><b>What history says.</b> House majorities that arrive with subpoena power and no veto-proof majority tend to spend the first year on oversight rather than legislation; 2011, 2019 and 2023 all followed that shape. It produces records and hearings rather than laws, and it is judged at the next election rather than by its outputs.</p>

<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Which arrives first with a date attached — a subpoena schedule or a legislative calendar. That single ordering tells you whether the new majority intends to govern or to investigate.</p>
