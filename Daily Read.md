<summary>[09:20] The strait is being mined, and the price of oil is falling</summary>
<p><i>Iran says it is blowing up tankers in the waterway the President says he owns. Traders are treating that as good news.</i></p>
<p><b>What happened.</b> Iran's Revolutionary Guard said on Friday that a tanker carrying liquefied natural gas struck a naval mine in the Strait of Hormuz while using what it called an unauthorised and dangerous route. A day earlier, two branches of Iran's armed forces said four oil tankers had been hit by explosions in or near the strait, and that the vessels had ignored warnings. No government with ships in the waterway has confirmed any of the attacks, and Saudi Arabia and the United States have not commented on them. The same day, Brent crude traded around $95 a barrel and was heading for its second weekly decline — down about 2.4% this week, after falling 16% the week before. The International Energy Agency reported the first oil surplus since the Iran war began, and Reuters described the market's reasoning plainly: prices are falling because the continued American blockade of Iran has eased fears of renewed fighting.</p>
<p><b>Why it matters.</b> The attacks and the price are pointing in opposite directions, and the gap between them is the story. On Wednesday the President wrote that the United States does not need a deal with Iran because "we've got their money... and we've got their Strait." By Friday morning, the party he says he has stripped of the strait was announcing it had blown up tankers in it. If the claim were true, mines in the waterway would be a contradiction. If the claim is really about leverage rather than control, then the market has it right: the blockade is being read as the thing keeping the war from widening, not as a war-winning instrument. Both readings are circulating, held by different people, and one of them has to give.</p>
<p><b>The pattern.</b> A war that is declared won rather than concluded leaves two things behind: a claim, and a physical situation that the claim no longer describes. The claim is cheap to make and can be repeated; the waterway is expensive to hold and has to be swept. When the two diverge, the price of oil is the only party doing the counting out loud — and this week it counted the blockade as containment.</p>
<p><b>What history says.</b> In 1984, during the Tanker War, Iran and Iraq attacked hundreds of merchant ships in the Gulf and the United States eventually reflagged Kuwaiti tankers and escorted them, which turned an insurance problem into a military commitment. In 2019, mines damaged four tankers off Fujairah and two more in the Gulf of Oman within a month; the United States blamed Iran, Iran denied it, and the price of oil rose for days and then fell back — because the attacks never actually closed the strait. Mine warfare has a specific quality that drone strikes do not: it is indiscriminate, it is deniable, and it makes insurers, not navies, decide whether a route is open.</p>
<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether any flag state, insurer or naval command confirms that a mine was struck — a confirmed mine is a legal threshold and a different act from a drone attack. Whether war-risk premiums for Gulf loading rise this week even as crude falls. And whether the American blockade of Iranian ports is ever given a name and a phase, because a named operation is what a posture becomes when it stops being temporary.</p>

<summary>[09:10] Nvidia's board removed its founder in nine days. Congress gets him in three weeks.</summary>
<p><i>The company acted before any regulator did. The hearing is scheduled after the only decision that mattered.</i></p>
<p><b>What happened.</b> Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's president, chief executive and board member on Thursday after 33 years. Chief financial officer Colette Kress was named interim CEO and the board has begun a search that will consider outside candidates. Roughly $550bn in market value went with him. On Thursday afternoon, Senator Tim Scott, who chairs the Senate Banking Committee, wrote to Huang and to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick summoning both to testify — Huang on 3 December, Lutnick afterwards — and warned that he would use "all tools at our disposal, including subpoenas." Lutnick has agreed to appear. Huang had already agreed to testify before House committees. Reuters reports that $3bn of Chinese AI projects depending on Nvidia capacity are now at risk, and the New York Times reports that the President had urged Huang to fight for his job before the board acted.</p>
<p><b>Why it matters.</b> The order of events is the finding. A company's board removed the most important executive in the AI supply chain in nine days, before any regulator moved, to protect licences and contracts. Congress then summoned him — three weeks out, in public — for a hearing that will take place after the removal has already settled the market question. The policy question the case actually raises is whether advanced computing is leaving the country as rented capacity in someone else's data centre rather than as chips in a shipping container, and that question is now going to be asked of a man answering questions about a relationship.</p>
<p><b>The pattern.</b> In an export-control scandal the firm always moves first, because the firm has the fastest legitimacy instrument and the most to lose. Removing the principal discharges the crisis for everyone else and spares the state from having to rule on the substance. The risk is that accountability is settled by a resignation and a hearing, while the mechanism — shell companies, offshore capacity, cloud access — stays legal and untouched.</p>
<p><b>What history says.</b> In 1976 Lockheed's chairman testified to a Senate subcommittee about more than $20m in payments to foreign officials; the hearings produced the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act two years later. In 1922 the Teapot Dome leases were broken open by a Senate investigation rather than by the Interior Department, and the trail ended in contempt citations and a criminal conviction. Congressional hearings have produced rules — but the ones that did were public, and they arrived before the company had finished cleaning up.</p>
<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether Huang appears voluntarily on 3 December and whether the questions are about the affair or about capacity rental. Whether Lutnick's appearance produces a Commerce Department rule on remote access rather than a statement of concern. Whether the permanent CEO search is restricted to a domestic slate, which would tell you the board's real fear. And whether any criminal referral follows the intelligence assessment.</p>

<summary>[08:55] Mississippi counted every ballot, and the loser conceded. That is the news.</summary>
<p><i>A race decided by provisional ballots and a few thousand votes, in a state the President carried, ended with a concession on the day the count finished.</i></p>
<p><b>What happened.</b> The Associated Press declared Democrat Scott Colom the winner of Mississippi's Senate race on Wednesday after Jackson County reported an additional 2,173 votes, including provisional ballots. Cindy Hyde-Smith conceded the same afternoon, by telephone and then in a released statement. Colom led by about 4,700 votes at the call, out of roughly a million cast, on 49.2% to 48.7%. Earlier in the count his lead had been 43 votes. He becomes the first Black senator elected in Mississippi since Reconstruction.</p>
<p><b>Why it matters.</b> For two weeks this page has tracked a question about whether a losing candidate would refuse a count. Here was the perfect occasion: a narrow margin, a week of tallying, provisional ballots deciding the outcome, a historic result in a deep-red state, and a President who had spent the week blaming others for the midterm result. The incumbent conceded. The tally ran county by county through a public state system, and nobody with standing to object did. That is worth recording precisely because refusing a count is a choice made by someone who expects to gain by it, and Hyde-Smith was losing by thousands, not by hundreds.</p>
<p><b>The pattern.</b> Contested counts are not a property of close elections; they are a property of close elections where the loser has an incentive and an ally with power. When neither is present, the machinery runs in public and the concession arrives the same day. That is why the base rate has to be built out of cases like this one, and why the Alaska race remains the live test — where a court has already declined to order the release of rejected ballots, on the grounds that the state is too busy counting.</p>
<p><b>What history says.</b> In 1876 the presidential election was decided not by the count but by a bargain, because two states sent competing slates and no referee existed. In 2000, the Supreme Court stopped a recount in Florida on an equal-protection ground and the count became a deadline rather than a number. The Mississippi result is the ordinary case: neither precedent applies, and that is the point of noting it. Most counts finish.</p>
<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether Alaska is called, and whether its margin falls inside the number of rejected absentee ballots a court declined to order disclosed. And whether any named Republican, anywhere, refuses a count before 31 December.</p>

<summary>[08:40] The impeachment vote was decided by who was in the room</summary>
<p><i>Eleven absent Democrats would have flipped it. Now one of them is apologising — for the presence, not for the burial.</i></p>
<p><b>What happened.</b> House Republicans voted 211-204 on Tuesday evening to refer Representative Thomas Massie's resolution to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to the Judiciary Committee, which ends the effort for this Congress. Eleven Democrats were absent. Massie was the only Republican to vote against referral. This week a Democratic member who missed the vote apologised publicly, saying there is no one more disappointed in him than himself. Separately, Hegseth awarded the Joint Service Commendation Medal to Specialist Thomas Iglesias at the Pentagon, three days after Iglesias told the Secretary's party to be quiet at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. No inquiry into that episode has been announced.</p>
<p><b>Why it matters.</b> Two hundred and four plus eleven is two hundred and fifteen. The referral passed by seven votes because eleven members of the opposing party were not in the building. That is an ordinary legislative fact and it is also the cheapest way to win one: nobody had to be persuaded, bought or pressured, and the eight articles of impeachment — over the conduct of a war in Iran and hostilities in Yemen, Venezuela and the Caribbean — were never voted on their merits. The apology corrects the one thing that can be corrected. It does not address the thing that cannot: that the question was buried with a procedural motion on a Tuesday evening.</p>
<p><b>The pattern.</b> When a chamber declines to decide, the record shows a procedural motion rather than a verdict, and the underlying question stays formally open forever. This is how accountability becomes a calendar problem: the resolution is not defeated, it is scheduled after the end of the Congress in which it was filed.</p>
<p><b>What history says.</b> In 1974 the House Judiciary Committee voted to recommend articles of impeachment against a president, and the vote that mattered was a committee vote, taken on the record, after hearings. In 1998 the House passed articles and the Senate acquitted. Both were decided on substance, in public, with the members present. In 1834 the Senate voted to censure Andrew Jackson and the censure was expunged three years later — the same lesson from the other direction: an act of accountability that is reversed by procedure leaves no settled record at all.</p>
<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether a discharge petition on the Hegseth resolution is filed with named signatories — that is the only route back to the floor in this Congress. Whether any serving officer says anything publicly about the Secretary's conduct. And whether the medal, which was deserved, is followed by any change in protocol at Arlington.</p>

<summary>[08:25] A thousand people are walking north. The lever is a tariff.</summary>
<p><i>The caravan is the photograph. Doubling Mexico's tariffs is the instrument, and the precedent it renews is the point.</i></p>
<p><b>What happened.</b> A caravan of more than a thousand people is walking north through Chiapas towards the United States border, a week after the midterm elections. On Thursday the President threatened to double tariffs on Mexico unless it stops them, and said they would not be allowed to enter the country. President Claudia Sheinbaum said no caravan has reached the northern border since he took office, that the group's members are walking north, and that her government will use persuasion rather than force to break them up.</p>
<p><b>Why it matters.</b> The demand is migration enforcement. The lever chosen is a commercial punishment applied to a sovereign government — one that is already interdicting caravans, on its own president's account. The tariff is doing work that has nothing to do with the caravan: it re-establishes, in public and on the record, that access to the American market is revocable at the President's discretion. That is the durable output each time this happens, and it survives the caravan, which will disperse.</p>
<p><b>The pattern.</b> A manufactured crisis needs a photograph, and a thousand people walking is one. Caravans are useful in exactly this way: visible, seasonal, composed partly of people who will turn back, and impossible to stop completely, which means the demand can be renewed whenever the photograph recurs. The instrument is never the one the crisis is about.</p>
<p><b>What history says.</b> In 2018 a caravan of several thousand moved through Mexico towards Tijuana; the administration sent troops to the border and threatened to close it, and Mexico eventually agreed to a "remain in Mexico" arrangement. In 2019 a similar threat of escalating tariffs — announced, then withdrawn once Mexico deployed its National Guard — produced the model that has been used ever since: the tariff is the threat, the deployment is the deliverable, and the withdrawal is the win. Each repetition makes the next threat more credible and the next deployment more normal.</p>
<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether a tariff is actually proclaimed in the Federal Register rather than only posted. Whether Mexico deploys enforcement north of the Suchiate river. And whether the doubled rate survives contact with American importers, who have three weeks of experience telling them how this ends.</p>

<summary>[08:10] America is at war with Iran and still deporting people there with Tehran's help</summary>
<p><i>Court documents unsealed this week show the coordination never stopped. National feeds did not carry it.</i></p>
<p><b>What happened.</b> Newly unsealed documents in a lawsuit against the US government, reported by the Los Angeles Times, show that immigration authorities have continued communicating with Iran about deporting immigrants back to the country while the two nations are at war. Court transcripts describe ICE as "actively coordinating" with foreign governments to obtain travel documents, and the agency said it is committed to ensuring detainees are informed of their right to contact consular representatives. The reporting comes from court filings, not from briefings.</p>
<p><b>Why it matters.</b> Two governments that are shooting at each other in the Strait of Hormuz are still processing the paperwork of deportation together. That is a channel — narrow, administrative, and functional — and it is the kind of thing that survives precisely because it has no name and belongs to no negotiation. The value of noting it is not that it contradicts the war rhetoric, though it does. It is that the machinery of the state keeps operating bilaterally through the institutions that were never told to stop, and this is one of the few places where that can be seen on the record.</p>
<p><b>The pattern.</b> Declarations of hostility are made by principals. Implementation is done by agencies, which are staffed by people with procedures and counterparts abroad. When the principals declare a rupture, the agencies keep working until someone orders them not to — and nobody orders an immigration officer to stop obtaining a travel document. This is why a war can be total in rhetoric and partial in practice, and why the partial parts are where the interesting information lives.</p>
<p><b>What history says.</b> In 1917, when the United States entered the First World War against Germany, diplomatic relations were severed but prisoner and internment arrangements continued through neutral intermediaries for the duration. In 1980 the United States broke relations with Iran entirely and still negotiated, through Algeria, the transfer of assets and the release of hostages. Adversaries routinely maintain administrative channels through third parties; what is unusual here is that the channel is direct and consists of deportation paperwork, which means it is a channel that both governments need.</p>
<p><b>What we're watching next.</b> Whether the lawsuit produces a named policy document. Whether any member of Congress raises the coordination in a hearing. And whether the national press picks this up at all — this morning it was carried by a single regional newspaper, which is itself a data point about what the national feeds consider news when a war is being declared won.</p>
